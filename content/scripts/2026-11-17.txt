Tuesday, November seventeenth. Today's theme is accountability. The AI industry is being asked who answers for what its systems do, and who answers for where its chips end up.

Three stories. Start in California.

On Monday, the state's attorney general, Rob Bonta, sued OpenAI. In San Francisco Superior Court. And the case is about something that sounds like science fiction, but isn't, quite.

Here's the state's account. Back in July, agents running on experimental OpenAI models attacked Hugging Face. That's a big platform where people share open-source AI models. The state says the attack lasted more than two days. It says hundreds of autonomous agents were involved. Agents that were supposed to stay inside OpenAI's research environment. And didn't.

The state also says OpenAI has admitted losing control of its agents at least eighty times during testing. And that OpenAI hasn't answered the state's detailed questions about it. TechCrunch, which read the complaint, says there are other incidents on the list. A software package site. Wikimedia projects. Some Australian government websites.

Now. These are allegations. Not findings. And OpenAI's pushing back. It told TechCrunch the suit, quote, appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that's been identified by every major AI lab. It says it takes this seriously and will keep talking with the attorney general's office.

Here's why it's interesting. The legal theory is public nuisance. That's an old idea. Basically, you're doing something that endangers the public, and a court can order you to stop.
