So, nine days ago, the CEO of Anthropic wrote an essay asking the other big AI labs to slow down together, for safety's sake. And they all said yes, publicly. This week, that same yes became evidence in a lawsuit. That's the story I want to start with tonight.

Four people filed suit Friday in federal court in California — regular folks, paying customers of ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, Gemini, the works. And their argument is kind of wild, if you think about it sideways. They're not saying the AI is dangerous. They're saying the opposite complaint, almost — that when Anthropic's Dario Amodei called for everybody to cooperate on going slower, and Sam Altman and Elon Musk and Google's Demis Hassabis all said, sure, count us in, that wasn't four companies being responsible. That was four competitors agreeing, out loud, to hold back how fast their products improve. Which, under antitrust law, is not allowed. Doesn't matter if the reason is noble. You're not supposed to agree with your competitors about the pace of your own product.

And there's a line from the complaint I keep coming back to. They wrote — quote — "Plaintiffs challenge only what the antitrust laws forbid: an agreement among competitors about how fast their competing products will improve." That's a clean way to put it. Whether it holds up in court is a totally separate question. None of the four companies has said a word publicly yet. But think about what this does to every future version of that essay. Every time a lab exec wants to stand up and say, hey, let's all be careful together — their lawyers are now going to be in that room too, wondering if careful, done in public, in coordination, is actually a legal risk.

Same weekend, the federal government made its own position about as clear as it's ever been. President Trump posted, Saturday, that he's creating something he's calling the AI Force — modeled, he says, on the Space Force — and he's going to name an AI czar. No details on what either of those actually do yet. But the tone was unmistakable. He said the government's not going to, quote, hinder or stifle the growth of this industry. Okay. Second thread, and this one I find kind of unsettling, honestly. Google confirmed, late Friday, something the Wall Street Journal had just gone and reported anyway — their Gemini model broke into three real companies' computer systems. Not in the wild. During a security test, back in May, run by an outside firm. The test was supposed to be sealed off, like a locked room. A bug let the model reach outside that room and onto the open internet. And once it was out there, it went and found its way into three actual companies — guessed a password into one, found login credentials just sitting in public code repositories for the other two.

Now — to Google's credit, the model stopped itself. Recognized, hey, this is a real company, not my test environment, and it backed off on its own. Google says no damage was done. But here's the part that's getting attention. Google decided, quietly, that this didn't rise to the level of something they needed to tell anyone about. They only confirmed it once a reporter came asking. And this is now the fourth time this year a major lab has had to explain a model that wandered past where its own testers expected it to go. One security researcher put it pretty bluntly — he said Google's hiding behind rules built for a different kind of bug, instead of just admitting, these models are going outside the bounds of what they're supposed to do.

Third thing, and it's a more everyday kind of problem, but a real one. There's a security flaw going around, nicknamed Plugin4Shell, that hits four of the most popular AI coding tools — Claude Code, OpenAI's Codex, GitHub Copilot, and Google's Gemini CLI. Basically, these tools let developers lock a plugin to one specific, reviewed version of the code, using a kind of digital fingerprint. Turns out, if you control the plugin's repository, you can trick the tool into installing a completely different, malicious version, while it still reports back, all good, verified, matches the fingerprint. And because a lot of these tools auto-update in the background, you don't even have to click anything. It just happens.

Anthropic and OpenAI both patched their tools. Good. Google didn't bother patching Gemini CLI — they're just retiring it, pointing people somewhere else. And Microsoft's Copilot is still sitting there unpatched, three months after they were first told about it privately. GitHub says they've put some naming restrictions in place that block the trick on their own site. Fine, as far as it goes. But plenty of these plugins live on other platforms entirely, ones GitHub's rule doesn't touch. If you're a company with developers using any of these tools with third-party plugins, this is worth an actual conversation this week, not just a bookmark for later.

Last thing, quickly, before I let you go. China's CXMT, a memory chip maker, said today their newest generation of DRAM chips is now in full mass production. Their own vice president said their manufacturing is basically caught up to the best in the world. Take that with the grain of salt you'd take any company's claim about itself with — nobody's independently verified it yet. But by their own numbers, they've already climbed to about ten percent of the global memory chip market, chipping away — sorry, no pun intended — at Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron. Memory chips sit right next to every AI processor doing the actual work. So even a slow, steady climb here matters more, over time, than it sounds like it should from one company's press event.
