Okay. Third story. This one's quieter, and I think it matters more to ordinary companies.

Fireworks AI is a company that hosts open AI models for developers. Other firms send their customers' text to it. On October twenty-third, a cloud provider that Fireworks used had a security incident. A day later, Fireworks says, someone used that to get into its development machines.

What did they get? Fireworks' own bulletin says billing and shipping addresses, invoice history, account balances. For a limited set of accounts, some secrets, datasets, and unfinished model weights. It says card numbers weren't stored, and weren't touched. It also says its main inference product shows no impact, because that one keeps no customer data.

Here's the part that reached regular people. Notion. Notion sent chat text from its AI feature to Fireworks. And Notion says some of those chat transcripts were taken. Fewer than one percent of workspaces, it says. And most of the transcripts couldn't be tied to a person or a company.

But chat text is chat text. People paste in things. Names, emails, sometimes a password or an API key. Notion's telling affected customers to rotate anything that showed up. It's turned that feature off, and it says it's auditing vendors on whether they keep any copy at all.

Fireworks says it's seen this before, too. TechCrunch says this is its second incident in two months. And its bulletin points to a known criminal tactic, where people steal access to AI models and resell it.

So the lesson is boring, and it's real. Your product is only as careful as the vendor you send your customers' words to.
