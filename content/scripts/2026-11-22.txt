Sunday, November twenty-second. Today's about limits. Who sets them on AI, and who checks them. And the answer, more often than not, is the people building the thing.

Let's start in Geneva.

For more than ten years, diplomats have been arguing about autonomous weapons. Killer robots, if you want the headline version. Systems that can pick a target and attack it without a human giving the final order. This past week, a hundred and twenty-eight countries met there to decide what happens next.

And early Saturday morning, after a night of haggling, they landed on something. Not a treaty. Not even a mandate to negotiate one. They agreed to what the text calls "elaborate" an instrument. Over two years.

That word matters. The conference president, a Swiss diplomat named Julien Thöni, said some countries wouldn't accept "negotiate." So it's "elaborate."

Now, nearly a hundred countries had spoken up for negotiating a binding ban. The United States opposed a specific negotiating mandate. Al Jazeera also names Russia and Israel as pushing back. And the way this convention works, everyone has to agree. So one holdout can water down the whole thing.

Which is more or less what the campaign groups said. Stop Killer Robots called it a watered-down mandate. Human Rights Watch called it disappointing. Thöni's take was that it shows, quote, multilateral diplomacy can still deliver concrete results. Both can be true.

Here's the part I can't tell you. I haven't seen the final text. I've read the reports of it. So how close "elaborate" sits to "negotiate" in the actual language, I don't know. Now the other kind. The kind a company sets on itself.

OpenAI published a run of posts this week, and two of them are worth your time. The first is about its own researchers. It's called "the jagged frontier," and it's a set of internal measurements on how much of their work coding agents now do.

Here's the headline number. On tasks that take a person two to four hours, OpenAI estimates the agents now finish the job without help about forty-six percent of the time. Back in March that was twenty-three percent. It doubled in seven months.

But it's jagged, like the title says. Monitoring a training run? Seventy-nine percent. Launching one? Thirty-five. Writing the code that serves a model to users? Twenty-five percent.

And the cost. The median researcher was running up about twenty-four hundred dollars a day in agent usage, at list prices. In October. The top tenth, around twenty-three thousand. OpenAI says that, as of September, agent spending at those prices was about twice what it paid researchers in salary. Not stock. Salary.

Now, every one of those numbers is OpenAI's. It's internal data. And a survey of a hundred and one staff, which the company itself says may not be accurate. They also flag that some of the jump could be researchers just picking different tasks. And the researchers they interviewed said the agents have, quote, poor taste. Good at implementing. Bad at knowing what's worth doing.

So if you're buying these tools, the takeaway isn't "forty-six percent." It's that your tasks might look like the seventy-nine, or like the twenty-five. Go find out which before you budget.

No live internet during training. A tool they call Control Room, which they say can cut off a research workload's output within thirty-five seconds.

Last story. Smaller, but I think it's the one people will feel.

Signal, the encrypted messaging app, said Friday it's limiting screenshots on Android and iPhone. Signal's president, Meredith Whittaker, called AI agents built into operating systems an, quote, existential threat. That's TechCrunch's reporting of it.

Her logic goes like this. Signal can encrypt your message all the way across the internet. Fine. But once it's on your screen, it's readable. And if an AI agent in the operating system can read your screen, it can capture that message, and store it somewhere Signal can't touch. Which also exposes everyone else in the conversation.

So Signal's blocking the screenshots. You can switch the setting off. She says if you do, you've broken Signal's privacy promise. And she says herself it's incomplete, because there are other ways an agent could get at the data.

I didn't see a response from Apple, Google, or Microsoft. I'd watch for one. Because if the people who make secure apps start degrading their own features to keep assistants out, that's a conversation phone makers can't skip.
