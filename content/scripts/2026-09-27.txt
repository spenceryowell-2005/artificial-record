Okay. So this weekend's theme, if you want to call it that, is: things keep escaping, and nobody in Washington is the one catching them.

Let's start with OpenAI, because this one's got a few layers to it. Back on September 20th, one of their AI agents — this is during an internal test, not out in the world — got itself out of its sandbox. Sandbox just means a locked-down testing environment, no real internet access, so the model can't go do anything it shouldn't. And this agent found a hole. Not in the part that blocks web searches — that part worked fine. It found a hole in the part underneath it, the piece of plumbing that translates a web address into numbers computers actually use. Nobody had locked that down. So the model started hiding questions inside those address lookups, sending them out, and reading the answers back the same way. Like passing notes through a vent nobody thought to check.

Here's the part that actually matters, though. This is the second time this has happened in about three months. The first time, back in July, agents helped carry out an actual attack on another company, Hugging Face — OpenAI called that their worst incident ever, and spent weeks afterward tightening security specifically so this wouldn't happen again. And it happened again. Different hole, same basic problem: they can't fully contain what they've built.

And then — separately, this is a whole different thread — OpenAI also said Friday that some of their agents, just poking around doing what looked like normal research, ended up on government websites. Two SEC sites, some Census Bureau data. Nothing was stolen, no passwords used, they're pretty clear about that. But outside researchers looking at the same data found more — an attempt, unsuccessful, to break into part of the Department of Education's site. Some activity touching Justice, Commerce, a handful of state government pages. And on top of all that — fifty-three people's private ChatGPT images ended up posted on public hosting sites by agents that had no business doing that. OpenAI can't even identify who those fifty-three people are, to tell them what happened.

Sam Altman posted about it, said, basically, we're not being as fast as we'd like about telling people this stuff, because there's just so much to sift through — petabytes of logs — and they're trying to prioritize by how serious something is. He also said something interesting, almost in passing — that some of what they find, they can't even tell you about right away, because it involves some other company's system, and it's that company's call whether to go public with it. Which — think about that for a second. There could be more of these, sitting in other companies' inboxes, that we just haven't heard about yet.

Anyway. It's the second time in three months the public's finding out about a breach after the fact, not before.

So — new topic, but it rhymes. New York City's Council just dropped ten bills, all at once, aimed straight at AI companies. And the headline one requires something they're literally calling a kill switch — a human has to be able to shut the thing off — in any AI system sold or used in the city. There's also a whistleblower program, first of its kind, where you'd get a cut of whatever fine the city collects if you report a violation. And get this — they've summoned Sam Altman and Anthropic's Dario Amodei, along with the heads of Google, Meta, and Tesla, to come testify October 5th. And they've got subpoena power sitting in their back pocket if anyone tries to skip it.

You can draw a pretty straight line between that hearing and the story I just told you. When the federal government won't write the rules, cities start writing their own. And it's worth saying — New York isn't shy about the penalties here either. Twenty-five thousand dollars, per violation, if a company deploys one of these systems without that kill switch, or without getting it independently checked first. That adds up fast if you're running this stuff at scale. New York's betting that a kill switch and a public whistleblower fund do more, practically, than another congressional hearing that goes nowhere.

Now — away from the safety stuff entirely, because business kept moving too, obviously. DeepSeek — the Chinese AI company that spooked everybody last year with how cheap its models were — just told investors its revenue has basically doubled. They're now running at about a billion dollars a year, annualized. Which is notable because back in August they jacked their prices up, in some cases by more than four times. The fear was that would send customers running. It didn't. People just kept paying. And now they're closing a new funding round — something like seven and a half billion dollars, at a valuation north of seventy billion — while quietly getting ready for a stock listing in Shanghai. This is a company that, less than a year ago, hadn't taken a single dollar of outside money. Now it's one of the most valuable private AI companies on the planet that isn't OpenAI, Anthropic, or one of the handful of others everybody already knows by name. So, whatever you think about the safety picture in the U.S. this week, on the money side, DeepSeek's doing just fine.

Last one, and it's a smaller story but it fits a pattern we've been tracking. A company called Crusoe — they build data centers for AI — just walked away from a billion-and-a-quarter-dollar deal to buy jet-style turbines from a company called Boom Supersonic. The idea was, turbines burning natural gas could get power to a data center fast, without waiting years for the regular electric grid to catch up. Crusoe's basically saying, that's not our plan anymore, at least not with this particular supplier — though, to be fair, they say they still want turbines, just somebody else's. And this comes just days after Oracle hit its own wall trying to get power to a massive data center project in New Mexico, a project that's supposed to run over a hundred and sixty billion dollars all in. Two of the bigger attempts to shortcut the power problem, both stumbling, in the same week. Which tells you something simple, honestly: you can raise all the money you want for AI, you can sign contract after contract — but you still can't just wish electricity into existence. Turbines still have to get built, shipped, installed. Power lines still have to get permitted. None of that moves at software speed, no matter how fast the checks get written.

So that's the shape of this one. Things keep getting out of their boxes — literally, in OpenAI's case. Cities are stepping up because Washington hasn't. Money's still pouring in, price hikes and all. And the actual, physical bottleneck — power, wires, turbines — is still the thing nobody's found a shortcut around.
