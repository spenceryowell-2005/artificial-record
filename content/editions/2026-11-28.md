## Top AI Headlines

### Sakana AI Says It Will Build Its Own Foundation Model, Sawara

**What happened.** TechCrunch published an interview on Nov. 28 with David Ha, co-founder and chief executive of Sakana AI, a Tokyo-based startup. Ha says the company announced in October that it would build its own foundation model, a large general-purpose AI model, named Sawara. Work starts in November 2026. A smaller model is targeted for early 2027, with a larger one to follow. Ha says the long-term aim is Japan's industrial and manufacturing sectors, especially "physical AI," meaning AI that controls machines in the real world. He says the smaller model should suit on-premises use, where a customer runs the model on its own hardware, with cybersecurity as a near-term need. On open-weight models, whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download, Ha says they are "biased towards their creators."

**Why it matters.** Sakana's current products build on other labs' models. According to TechCrunch, Fugu, launched about five months ago, is an orchestration system that combines other large language models, including from US labs, to build task-specific agents, and it uses OpenAI's models. Namazu fine-tunes open-weight models from Meta and DeepSeek for Japanese language and business use. Sawara would be the first model the company trains itself. TechCrunch also reports that Sakana signed a research contract with Japan's Ministry of Defense in July 2026 on AI that analyzes and combines information from multiple sources. It has worked with the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications on detecting disinformation. Ha links the model plan to those needs: "for national defense," he says, Japan will want its own systems.

**Business implication.** This is a stated plan, not a release. The article gives no funding figure, no investor names, no valuation and no model size, so we cannot say what Sawara will cost or how it will perform. On a possible listing, TechCrunch says Nikkei and Bloomberg reported IPO speculation earlier this month. We did not open those reports. Ha says there are no near-term plans and that "we have a gravitational pull to eventually IPO in Japan." For Japanese manufacturers and agencies, the practical question is whether a domestic model that can run on their own hardware arrives early enough to matter. For US labs, it is another sign that governments and large customers want models they do not depend on a foreign vendor to supply.

*Sources: [TechCrunch, Nov. 28](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/28/sakana-ais-ceo-says-its-homegrown-models-are-about-more-than-japans-national-security/). We did not locate a Sakana AI announcement of Sawara; the details rest on TechCrunch's interview.*

### OpenAI Publishes Dean Ball's "The Silent Consensus"

**What happened.** On Nov. 18, OpenAI published "The Silent Consensus," an essay by Dean Ball in its "Intelligence Age" series. An author's note says it is adapted from a lecture at Harvard and reflects Ball's views, not necessarily those of OpenAI or its employees. Ball argues that many people in AI quietly expect humans to give up control of the economy and institutions to machines. "I call it the silent consensus," he writes. He says no AI revolt is needed. Each decision to delegate work can make sense by itself, and if AI outperforms people, market pressure could push humans out of control. He calls the result "the termination of human primacy in the world as we know it." He rejects that outcome, and says he believes humans can stay in control while admitting the case is hard to make.

**Why it matters.** The essay is an argument, not a finding. It contains no statistics. Its proposals are general: hold developers accountable for unsafe practices, and hold people and firms accountable for misuse or poor oversight of AI. Build technical systems that tie people to their agents' actions. Give agents "intelligible, limited, auditable" permissions that users can withdraw. Grant agents the ability to form corporations, own property or sue "with extraordinary caution, if at all." Ball says he would "distrust anyone who claimed to possess" a complete blueprint. These are positions on questions that courts and legislators have only started to take up, and a major lab chose to publish them.

**Business implication.** None of this changes a rule today. For companies deploying agents, it is a preview of the questions regulators and customers may ask: who is accountable for an agent's actions, what it is permitted to do, and whether a user can switch it off. The ShellBench study we covered yesterday found that agents that got every correctness criterion right still failed a safety requirement in 9.9% of those runs, which is a practical reason to take permission design seriously. We note that the essay is published by a company that sells agents, under a disclaimer that it does not speak for the company.

*Sources: [OpenAI, "The Silent Consensus," Nov. 18](https://openai.com/index/the-silent-consensus/); [OpenAI, "Perplexity and Intelligence" (Nov. 22, covered in a prior edition)](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/).*
