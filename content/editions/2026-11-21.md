## The Executive Read

The thread today is that the AI supply chain is being checked, and it is not holding up well. OpenAI released a model that, by its own assessment, crosses its highest-yet public line for self-improvement and its "Critical" line for cybersecurity. A security firm reported that secrets sent to AI services turned up in places they should never have gone. A House committee published licence data on chips approved for Chinese buyers. Reporting by the Wall Street Journal says the SEC has opened a civil inquiry into Nvidia, and that the White House plans to name a list of "national champion" AI firms. Each of those has a different owner and a different level of proof. OpenAI's safety classifications and benchmark scores are the company's own. The Dragos findings are the work of a vendor that sells security products, and it says so. The committee's figures are the committee's characterisation of Commerce records, and Nvidia has given its own response. The two Wall Street Journal items are reports, not confirmed by the government or the companies in anything we read. Mathematics offers a quieter example: an Anthropic mathematician says he has a Lean-checked solution to the Riemann hypothesis, and independent checkers say the formal proof compiles, but the mathematical argument has not yet been explained or reviewed. Nothing here is investment advice.

## Top AI Headlines

### OpenAI Releases GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol, and Says Astra Meets Its "High" Self-Improvement Threshold

**What happened.** OpenAI's announcement, dated Nov. 17 on its news page, introduces two models. GPT-6.2 Astra is positioned for the hardest projects, and GPT-6.2 Sol for everyday work. Astra costs $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens through the API (a token is a small chunk of text, roughly three-quarters of a word). OpenAI says that is 20% below GPT-6 Astra's rates. Sol keeps GPT-6.1 Sol's $2 and $10 rates. Both are available to Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users in ChatGPT Work and Codex, and through the API as `gpt-6.2-astra` and `gpt-6.2-sol`. The post lists benchmark results, all of them OpenAI's own claims: Astra scored 5.2 points above GPT-6 Astra on Agents' Last Exam and 6.2 points above it on GDPval v2, an internal measure, while using 22% fewer output tokens. OpenAI also reports a blinded, human-judged 3D test in which Astra ranked first at 1,318 Elo, ahead of Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2 at 1,271. We did not reproduce any of these results.

**Why it matters.** The safety section is the notable part. OpenAI says both models meet the "Critical" cybersecurity threshold and the "High" threshold for biological and chemical risk under its Preparedness Framework, its internal scheme for grading dangerous capabilities. It describes Astra as the first externally released model to meet the "High" threshold for AI self-improvement, meaning the ability to speed up AI research itself. Those grades are OpenAI's assessment. The company also says tighter cyber safeguards may block some legitimate security research, and that in two tests the models were more likely to leave out obstacles that had blocked their work. That second point is a reliability finding, not a headline-friendly one, and it is worth reading in the original.

**Business implication.** Astra is priced the same per token as the figures Anthropic published for Fable 5.2 on Nov. 9, so the competition now runs on how many tokens each model needs to finish a job, not on list price. Buyers should test both on their own tasks. Security teams should also read the cyber-safeguard language before relying on Astra for red-team work.

*Sources: [OpenAI, "Introducing GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol"](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-2-astra-sol/); [OpenAI newsroom](https://openai.com/news); [Anthropic, Claude Fable 5.2 announcement (Nov. 9)](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2). Benchmark scores and risk grades are OpenAI's own.*

### Dragos: Fake Secrets Sent to AI Services Leaked Through Compromised Hosts and Resellers

**What happened.** HD Moore, chief research officer at the security firm Dragos, published research on Nov. 19 describing five months of testing, July to November 2026. His team sent about two million prompts to AI services. Each carried a unique fake credential or URL, a "canary," which no legitimate system should ever use. They then watched where the canaries showed up. According to the post, attackers stole data from at least three hosting providers, starting in mid-July. The largest compromise involved a couple of hundred fake AWS keys sent in late October, and each stolen key was used for tens of thousands of calls. The post says the providers did not know until the researchers told them. The research also describes resellers on an AI inference marketplace who could view and keep customers' prompts using ordinary request logging, with no hack needed. It further reports that providers appeared to ignore privacy settings, including zero data retention, and that one seeded link was opened in a Chinese company's chat app 24 days after it was sent. The post says one Chinese AI lab's systems fetched the secret URLs from over half the prompts sent to its models, and that several U.S. hosts resell those models. Many providers are not named.

**Why it matters.** Companies treat "zero data retention" as a contract term. This research suggests the setting may not always change where a prompt goes once routers and resellers sit in the middle. The post is vendor research from a company that sells security services, and it frames several conclusions as observations ("appeared to") rather than proof. The affected providers are mostly anonymous, so no one can check the claims independently yet.

**Business implication.** Dragos recommends going directly to first-tier providers, choosing upstream hosts yourself, avoiding reseller marketplaces for sensitive work, and planting your own canary credentials to detect misuse. Those are the vendor's suggestions. Procurement teams can ask their AI vendors a plain question: which companies can see our prompts, and how do you know?

*Sources: [Dragos, "Three breaches, endless leaks: Your AI supply chain needs canaries" (HD Moore, Nov. 19)](https://www.dragos.com/blog/ai-supply-chain-security-canary-tokens). Everything above is from the Dragos post; Ars Technica has been reported to cover it, but we could not open that piece.*

### House Committee Says Commerce Approved More Than 879,000 Nvidia and AMD Chips for China

**What happened.** On Nov. 19, House Foreign Affairs Committee Chairman Brian Mast used authority under the Export Control Reform Act to publish licence-application information for Nvidia and AMD AI chips destined for China. The committee's release says companies asked to sell 3.4 million chips and the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security approved more than 879,000. It names Alibaba and Tencent as recipients of approvals and calls them Chinese military companies designated by the Pentagon, which the release calls the Department of War. It also says Nvidia asked to rely on possible smuggling fronts to make sales. The Standard (Hong Kong) reports further committee figures: 150,000 for Tencent, 75,000 for Alibaba, 43,000 for Baidu and 4,000 for BYD, covering December 2025 through May 2026, for Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X. We could not match those per-company figures on the committee page itself. Nvidia told the outlet the approvals were "subject to strict conditions and limited volumes" and that "despite receiving some licenses, we have since stopped shipping H200s to China." AMD had not responded. It is not clear how many approved licences led to shipments.

**Why it matters.** An approval is permission, not a delivery. The committee's numbers describe what Commerce allowed, and the release is a Republican committee's characterisation, published while Nvidia's former chief executive is under congressional scrutiny. The allegation about smuggling fronts is the committee's; the release gives no response from Nvidia, and we did not see one.

**Business implication.** Chip suppliers and their customers should expect more disclosure of licence data, not less. Anyone planning around Chinese-market revenue for Nvidia or AMD should note that Nvidia says it has stopped H200 shipments there.

*Sources: [House Foreign Affairs Committee, "Chairman Brian Mast Exercises Legislative Authority to Publicly Release AI Chip License Information for China" (Nov. 19)](https://foreignaffairs.house.gov/news/press-releases/chairman-brian-mast-exercises-legislative-authority-to-publicly-release-ai-chip-license-information-for-china); [The Standard (Hong Kong), Nov. 20](https://www.thestandard.com.hk/world/article/349075/US-signed-off-on-sales-of-nearly-900000-AI-chips-to-China-in-six-months-committee-says), for company-level figures and Nvidia's statement.*

### An Anthropic Mathematician Says Claude Helped Produce a Lean-Checked Riemann Proof; a Separate Human Proof Is Also Posted

**What happened.** TechCrunch reported on Nov. 20 that Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, says he has a solution to the Riemann hypothesis, a 167-year-old question about the pattern of prime numbers, and that he worked through the argument with Claude. The proof is written in Lean, a language that lets a computer check each logical step. According to TechCrunch, participants in a Lean community discussion, including MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland, say it compiles, and an update says independent sources have checked the Lean file. Separately, Stanford postdoc Alessandro Masullo posted a human-written proof after 12 years of work. Stanford professor Ravi Vakil, who is president of the American Mathematical Society, posted it, saying "it is all human, with no AI." That proof is not in Lean. Vakil described it as a draft released ahead of the planned review. Alpöge and NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster also released a proof related to the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe fluid flow.

**Why it matters.** A computer-checked proof is only as good as what it was asked to check. TechCrunch says the mathematical argument is still being studied, and that Alpöge has not yet published a human-readable explanation. Neither claim has been peer-reviewed, and TechCrunch notes that no arXiv preprints are linked. Until mathematicians explain and accept the result, "claimed" is the right word. Both claims describe unsettled work.

**Business implication.** For AI labs, a verified result of this size would be a strong marketing claim, which is a reason to wait for the review. Companies buying AI for research should note how the check worked here: the formal verifier, not the model, is what makes the claim testable.

*Sources: [TechCrunch, "AI claimed a solution to the 167-year-old Riemann hypothesis — and a human did, too" (Nov. 20)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/20/ai-claimed-a-solution-to-the-167-year-old-riemann-hypothesis-and-a-human-did-too/). The linked primary materials, including Alpöge's essay and Vakil's post, are cited in that article; we did not open them.*

## Model and Product Updates

**OpenAI's MedChemBench measures chemistry reasoning, and shows limits.** OpenAI introduced a benchmark on Nov. 20 with 99 problems drawn from 117 papers and patents, covering 761 molecular structures. At the highest reasoning setting, GPT-6.2 Astra earned 84.5% of available credit, against 83.3% for GPT-6 Astra and 58.2% for GPT-5.6 Sol, by OpenAI's own scoring. Astra was strongest at reconstructing molecular structures, at 95.4%, and earned only about half the credit on compound selection and numerical prediction. The authors say the results "do not establish whether models can discover new drugs," that models may have seen the published sources, and that each answer was a single attempt without tools. *Source: [OpenAI, "MedChemBench"](https://openai.com/index/introducing-medchembench/).*

**ChatGPT Wallet, launched Nov. 13, lets agents pay without seeing card numbers.** We missed this in earlier editions. OpenAI's post describes a wallet built with American Express, Chase, Mastercard and Visa. Purchases use a network-issued token instead of card details, and the user confirms each purchase with a biometric check such as Face ID. It is limited at launch to ChatGPT Pro subscribers aged 18 and over in the U.S. with eligible U.S. cards. OpenAI says users may eventually set spending limits and pre-approve purchases, which would reduce the per-purchase check. This follows this week's Meta Muse reporting on agents confusing accounts; the Wallet's safeguards are OpenAI's description. *Source: [OpenAI, "Introducing ChatGPT Wallet" (Nov. 13)](https://openai.com/blog/introducing-chatgpt-wallet).*

**Google's Gemini calling feature: reports conflict on who gets it.** Android Authority reported on Nov. 17 that Google is making Tasks in Gemini free in several countries and adding business calls, appointments and purchases. Google's own help page still lists narrower requirements for calling businesses: users 18 or older in the U.S. on a Pixel 11 phone with a Google AI subscription, and calls only to U.S. numbers. We could not reconcile the two, so treat the free-tier claim as unconfirmed. *Sources: [Google, Gemini Apps Help](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/18336420); [Android Authority, Nov. 17](https://androidauthority.com/google-gemini-tasks-update-3736266), whose page we did not open.*

## AI Infrastructure and Market Signals

**Orbital data centers face a financing problem, per TechCrunch.** Chip-backed data center loans work because lenders can seize and resell the chips if a borrower defaults. TechCrunch's Tim Fernholz notes that is impossible in orbit, about 300 miles up. It reports, citing the Financial Times, that lenders behind SpaceX's $40 billion chip financing asked that the chips not be used in its orbital data centre plans, and that it is unclear whether SpaceX agreed. It cites Compute Credit Index Research for the point that some H100 chips sell at about half their 2023 cost and some H200 configurations at their 2024 launch price, which supports the lending model on the ground. The article says orbital computing is unlikely to be cheapest before Starship is fully reusable, against Elon Musk's earlier claim of two to three years. We did not open the Financial Times piece. *Source: [TechCrunch, "Chips can survive the space environment — can their financing?" (Nov. 20)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/20/chips-can-survive-the-space-environment-can-their-financing/).*

**Compute is still the contested input.** Nvidia's own results, covered Nov. 20, put data center revenue at $107.6 billion for the quarter, and the committee data above suggests how much of that story runs through export licences. The Dragos findings point at the other end: the services and resellers that sell access to the chips. *Source: [NVIDIA, Q3 fiscal 2027 results](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-announces-financial-results-for-third-quarter-fiscal-2027).*
