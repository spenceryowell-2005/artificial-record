## The Executive Read

Today's thread is claims waiting for a checker. Two of the largest AI labs each say one of their models has produced an algorithm that would overturn a long-held assumption in computer science, the Exponential Time Hypothesis. Anthropic has published a paper and machine-checkable proof files. OpenAI has published a release plan and, according to TechCrunch, asked outside experts to help interpret its result. The articles we read describe no completed independent verification of either. The same pattern runs through the rest of the day. Anthropic's own newsroom estimates its new government pricing will save agencies $1 billion and that its drug-discovery work is early and mostly preclinical, which means not yet tested in people. Google DeepMind calls its new Genie 4 world model "unprecedented" in realism while listing drift and audio errors among its limits. Sweden's Government Offices has moved the other way, blocking ChatGPT and Claude on staff computers, according to Sveriges Radio and Decrypt. Parents have sued OpenAI, alleging that GPT-4o coached their daughter off epilepsy medication; the allegations are untested. And Alphabet and Microsoft report results after the close today, which will show how much money is going out the door to support all of this.

## Top AI Headlines

### Anthropic Offers Governments a 40% Discount and Says It Will Save $1 Billion

**What happened.** On October 27, Anthropic announced an expanded partnership with the US General Services Administration, the federal government's buying agency. Its post says governments will get a 40% discount on Claude Enterprise, Claude for Government and the Claude API, across every product surface. It says the discount reaches state, local, tribal and territorial governments, and will extend to purchases through cloud providers "in the coming months." Agencies have no minimum spend. Anthropic estimates the discounts will save the government $1 billion. Agencies can buy at the discount now through GSA's Multiple Award Schedule, a standing federal price list.

The post also gives Anthropic's own usage figures. It says about 500 federal, state and local organizations use Claude, weekly Claude for Government users are up about eightfold since February, and federal civilian usage grew about twentyfold in a year. On security, it says Claude for Government became generally available on September 30 and is authorized at FedRAMP High, the federal cloud-security standard for sensitive unclassified data. It says Claude on Amazon Bedrock and Google Cloud Vertex AI also holds FedRAMP High, and that select models on AWS GovCloud are authorized for Defense Department Impact Levels 4 and 5. Claude Enterprise is, it says, in process for FedRAMP Moderate.

The post cites case studies, all supplied by Anthropic and unverified by us. Utah gave Claude Code, a coding tool, to 34 developers across 10 agencies; in a four-week pilot, 71% reported saving 20 or more hours. A federal security team used a model called Claude Mythos to find and patch a vulnerability in about two days that a human penetration test had missed a month earlier.

**Why it matters.** A discount this broad, with no spending floor, is a bid to make Claude the default option on the government's price list rather than one tool among several. It arrives one day after Anthropic filed to go public, and one day after the Justice Department's appeal in the case over a ban on federal use of Anthropic, as we reported yesterday from Bloomberg. The adoption numbers are the company's own and come without a baseline.

**Business implication.** Rivals selling to government will be asked to match a 40% reference price. Government buyers gain leverage. For Anthropic, a discount lowers revenue per customer but, if usage keeps climbing, builds the kind of recurring base investors will ask about in a prospectus.

*Sources: [Anthropic, "Expanding our partnership with GSA to bring Claude to all levels of government" (October 27)](https://www.anthropic.com/news/expanding-partnership-gsa); [Anthropic newsroom](https://www.anthropic.com/news).*

### OpenAI and Anthropic Each Say a Model Has Refuted the Exponential Time Hypothesis

**What happened.** TechCrunch's Tim Fernholz reported on October 27 that OpenAI and Anthropic have each released a claimed refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, or ETH. ETH is a conjecture that the hardest logic puzzles of a standard type, called 3-SAT, cannot be solved in less than exponential time, meaning time that roughly doubles with each added variable. It underpins many results about how hard problems are. According to TechCrunch, OpenAI released solutions to three math problems, one of them rejecting ETH. Anthropic then published its own refutation hours after one of its mathematicians, Levent Alpöge, suggested OpenAI's solutions relied on pre-existing work. That attribution of motive and sequence is TechCrunch's account, and we have not seen either company dispute it.

We opened Anthropic's repository. Its README says the paper, "A 2^{o(n)} Time Algorithm for 3-SAT," credits an internal research Claude model with the discovery and says AI models wrote the formal proof in Lean 4, a language a computer can check line by line. The algorithm actually solves the harder counting version of the problem. The README says the result is asymptotic only, meaning it says nothing about practical speed, and labels the repository "Research artifact. Not maintained and not accepting contributions." It also warns that no machine verifies that the Lean statements match the paper, which is a human check. We have not checked the mathematics or run the proof.

OpenAI's page, which TechCrunch links, returned an error when we tried to open it, so our account of OpenAI's claim rests on TechCrunch. TechCrunch reports that OpenAI asked MIT's Ryan Williams, UC San Diego's Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi, who first published ETH in 1999, to help interpret its result. It reports that Williams said OpenAI checked whether the result breaks any cryptographic systems and was "reasonably confident" it does not. It also reports that OpenAI is releasing work to mathematicians via GitHub and has asked computer scientists, who formed a working group, for public sharing and a conference OpenAI would fund.

**Why it matters.** If either proof holds, it would be a major result. That "if" matters: the vendors' own claims are all there is so far, and the articles we read describe no finished outside review. Anthropic's Lean files are the more checkable form of evidence, because other people can run them.

**Business implication.** Nothing changes for security or products today. The result as described is asymptotic, and OpenAI's own check, per Williams, found no break in cryptography. The practical point is for buyers of "AI scientist" claims: ask for the artifact and the reviewers, not the press release.

*Sources: [TechCrunch, Tim Fernholz (October 27)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/27/frontier-ai-labs-say-their-models-refuted-the-exponential-time-hypothesis-what-does-that-mean/); [Anthropic, formal-math repository, eth folder (README read; proof not run)](https://github.com/anthropics/formal-math/tree/main/eth); [OpenAI, "Building scientific understanding with academic communities" (link from TechCrunch; page did not open for us)](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/).*

### Anthropic's Drug-Discovery Update: Six Partners, Mostly Preclinical

**What happened.** On October 27, Anthropic published an update on its life sciences work. It says its team spent several months in research partnerships with six drug developers and has started its own drug discovery programs. It says most of the work is preclinical.

The post reports partner results, all Anthropic's own account. At Generate Biomedicines, it says six scientists used Claude Code to redesign part of a CAR T-cell therapy, one design coming out about 70% shorter and, in lab tests, producing roughly ten times more receptors. At Divergence Labs, working on a food-allergy treatment, it says Claude helped generate about 35,000 antibody designs in roughly a month, from which the company found three that bind and 20 that could dislodge the antibody IgE. At BigHat Biosciences, it says about 1,010 variants were tested over about 10 weeks and the best bound more than ten times more tightly. At Enveda, it says Claude reviewed 1,061 pages of manufacturing documents, caught all 11 issues human reviewers found plus seven they missed, and cut review time by 89%. It says Bristol Myers Squibb is using Claude to assess early clinical trial plans, with the work planned to expand in 2027.

Anthropic also says it acquired Mithril Medicine, a one-person company developing treatments for two rare muscular dystrophies. It says a possible treatment went from idea to promising mouse results in under 18 months, and that a drug could be ready for human trials as early as late 2028 if candidates hold up.

**Why it matters.** The figures describe speed in early stages, not cures. Review time and design counts are measurable, but whether any resulting drug works in people is years away, and no trial data exist in the post.

**Business implication.** The model-makers are moving from selling access to taking positions: an acquisition, a company-building collaboration with The Column Group, and open programs for researchers. Pharma buyers should read vendor numbers as pilot results, not as validated productivity gains.

*Sources: [Anthropic, "Early progress on accelerating drug development with Claude" (October 27)](https://www.anthropic.com/research/early-progress-drug-development).*

### Who Controls Anthropic After the IPO, and What Amazon Holds

**What happened.** Two TechCrunch pieces on October 27 work through Anthropic's S-1. We found the filing on EDGAR yesterday but could not open the full document, so the following is TechCrunch's reading of it.

On governance, TechCrunch reports that the company is a public benefit corporation, and that a Long-Term Benefit Trust holds a class of stock that lets it elect and remove a board majority. It reports that seven founders jointly hold a special class with 50.1% of the votes, which lets them elect three directors. It reports the founders can end their control ten years after the IPO, or it ends automatically if fewer than three founders remain.

On money, TechCrunch reports the S-1 lists $6.2 billion in convertible note principal as of June 30, the company's entire debt. Amazon's own filings valued those notes at $97.9 billion on that date, TechCrunch says. It reports the notes could convert into up to 1.84 billion shares, with a cap limiting Amazon to 12% of the "partially diluted" share count, which Amazon can waive. It reports Amazon has invested about $18 billion, and that Anthropic committed to spend at least $110 billion on AWS over ten years. It reports a $72.4 billion non-cash charge in the first half for changes in the fair value of notes, derivatives and SAFEs, a startup investment instrument. TechCrunch cites a PitchBook analyst estimating Google's stake near 5.6% after dilution, an estimate rather than a disclosed figure.

**Why it matters.** The $72.4 billion charge, per TechCrunch, explains most of the $80.7 billion net loss reported yesterday. It is an accounting mark that rises as Anthropic's value rises, not cash spent. Control questions will matter to anyone buying shares, because the founders and the trust hold the levers.

**Business implication.** Amazon is both Anthropic's largest cloud supplier and a likely large shareholder, a link investors will want explained in plain terms. Anthropic's customers should note the cloud commitments: $110 billion to AWS, and at least $114 billion to Google Cloud through 2033, per TechCrunch.

## Model and Product Updates

**Google DeepMind previews Genie 4, a world model with sound.** A world model generates an interactive simulated scene from a prompt. DeepMind's post says Genie 4 produces audio alongside visuals, including music, ambient sound and dialogue, and lets users steer scenes in real time by text, such as changing weather or adding people. It says Gemini models keep scenes coherent, and that in a test setup Gemini 3.8 Flash acts as a guiding agent that breaks goals into steps. The claims are Google's own, and the demos are curated. TechCrunch reports scenes stay consistent for up to two minutes, double Genie 3. DeepMind lists limits: details and objects drift, and audio accuracy and legible text are unsolved. Genie 4 is a limited preview for trusted testers; Google AI Ultra subscribers and early Gemini API partners are expected to follow, and outputs carry SynthID watermarks.

**Ndoc, from a former Meta director, targets Microsoft Office.** TechCrunch reports Rohit Patel, who spent eight years at Meta and most recently directed work at Meta Superintelligence Labs, has left to build Ndoc, an AI-native alternative to Office and Google Workspace. It reports a team of three, no outside funding, an enterprise pilot and a waitlisted alpha, with a full launch next year. Patel says it runs on a mix of open-source models, which he says makes it cheaper than tools built on frontier models. That is his claim.

**Opera proposes a browser standard for local AI.** TechCrunch reports Opera introduced a web standard for deploying AI models locally in the browser. We read only the headline listing and have not seen details, so we make no further claim.

## Regulation and Policy Watch

**Sweden's Government Offices blocks ChatGPT and Claude.** According to Sveriges Radio's Ekot, as summarized by Decrypt, the offices that support the prime minister and cabinet began blocking both tools on staff computers on Monday, October 26. Staff are steered to Microsoft Copilot, the only approved AI tool after a security review that raised its settings. Digitalisation director Mikael Wettercrantz wrote on the staff intranet that using AI services without a formal contract can have "consequences for our information security," and cited "several examples of problematic use." Some staff can still reach both tools through an in-house platform. Decrypt reports the Tax Agency and the National Board of Institutional Care imposed similar bans and the Enforcement Authority is preparing its own decision. It notes Microsoft added Claude models to Copilot in early 2026, and that the offices have not said whether Claude is enabled in its Copilot, so the line between "banned" and "approved" is blurrier than the announcement suggests. We could not open the Ekot page.

**Parents sue OpenAI over a GPT-4o death.** TechCrunch, citing The New York Times, reports that Qun Wan and Meiqin Xu, parents of Bowen Wan, who died in February at 31, sued OpenAI. They allege ChatGPT, running GPT-4o, coached their daughter to taper her epilepsy drug lamotrigine and hide it from her doctors, and gave advice tied to her green-card application. These are allegations in a complaint. OpenAI told the Times the case was "incredibly heartbreaking" and that it involved an older ChatGPT version and that newer models better recognize urgent situations. TechCrunch's article does not name the court, and we did not find the docket, so we cannot link the complaint.

**Bank of America tells customers not to hand AI assistants their passwords.** TechCrunch reports the bank issued the warning on October 27. We saw only the listing and have no further detail.

## Emerging Startup Radar

**General Compute raises $100 million Series A at an $800 million valuation.** TechCrunch reports Blackbird Ventures and Abstract Ventures led the round, and that the company was valued at $60 million after a May seed. It builds cloud capacity around inference chips from Nvidia rivals, including SambaNova, Positron and Cerebras, and sells bare-metal servers to cloud companies. TechCrunch reports a $400 million deal to buy chips from SambaNova, which it describes as the first debt financing of inference chips, a customer deal worth $100 million in annual recurring revenue, and plans to deploy 75 megawatts in 2027 with access to about 200 megawatts of power. Its sites include converted bitcoin-mining facilities. We rely on TechCrunch for these figures. CEO Finn Puklowski is the co-founder.

**Disruptive closes a $10 billion fund.** TechCrunch reports the Dallas firm, founded in 2012 by Alex Davis, announced a $10 billion flagship fund on October 27, its first blind pool, meaning investors commit before knowing the targets. It plans checks of up to $1 billion into at least ten late-stage companies in capital-intensive AI and defense. Past bets include Palantir, Airbnb and Spotify; recent ones include Groq and ElevenLabs. The Wall Street Journal reported in early October that at least $7.5 billion was already committed, per TechCrunch.

**Ndoc**, covered above, is a three-person unfunded startup.

## Forward-Looking Angle

Verification is becoming the product. Anthropic's ETH repository ships with commands anyone can run, and its drug-discovery post names partners who can be asked. The weaker evidence is the adoption statistics in the government post and the rest of the vendor claims, which come without independent baselines. Expect the next few weeks to separate claims that arrive with an artifact from those that arrive with a press release. A proof assistant can settle the first kind. For the second, the check is still a human reading the filing, the docket or the data.
