## Top AI Headlines

### The FCC Authorizes SpaceX's "Starmind" Orbital Data Center Constellation

**What happened.** The FCC's Space Bureau adopted and released an order on Nov. 25 granting SpaceX authority to operate a constellation of up to 1,000,000 satellites as orbital data centers, under the name Starmind in the order. The order covers altitudes from 500 to 1,002 kilometers. Primary communication between satellites is to be by optical (laser) links. The FCC also authorized radio-frequency channels in the Ka-band, a range of satellite radio frequencies, for backup tracking and command, on an unprotected basis: SpaceX may not claim protection from interference and must stop any transmission that causes documented harmful interference. The license runs 15 years from the date SpaceX certifies its first satellite is operating. The order sets milestones, which the document says make the authorization void if missed: one satellite operating by Nov. 25, 2033; 10% of the maximum by Nov. 25, 2035; 50% by Nov. 25, 2038; and the full constellation by Nov. 25, 2040. The bureau denied petitions filed by Viasat, Amazon's Kuiper Systems (Amazon Leo) and the American Astronomical Society, and said some of the astronomers' environmental concerns fall outside its authority. TechCrunch quotes FCC Chair Brendan Carr saying the action "opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America's leadership and growth."

**Why it matters.** The order is narrower than a headline reading of "FCC approves a million satellites" suggests. The bureau deferred SpaceX's request for a waiver of the rule that satellites ending missions below 2,000 kilometers re-enter the atmosphere within five years. SpaceX wanted room to move some retired satellites above 600 kilometers into other orbits, including around the Sun. Before launching any satellite that will not re-enter, SpaceX must file a modification with a justification. The bureau found the disposal plan adequate for satellites operating below 600 kilometers. The order also requires semiannual reports on close approaches with other objects, re-entries and the number of satellites deployed. TechCrunch notes that approval does not mean orbital data centers are close. It lists lower manufacturing and launch costs, cooling processors in space, and financing chips headed for orbit as open problems. The FCC grants permission to use spectrum and orbits. It does not test whether the computing works.

**Business implication.** The first operating satellite is not due for about seven years, so this is a long-dated option, not a near-term capacity source. Amazon, which opposed the plan, and other satellite operators now face a competitor with regulatory standing in the same orbits. TechCrunch reports Amazon called the proposal a "lofty ambition rather than a real plan" in its filing; we did not read the petition. Data center developers weighing land and power constraints on the ground should note that nothing in the order changes the 2026 or 2027 picture.

*Sources: [FCC Space Bureau order, DA 26-1273, Nov. 25](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-1273A1.txt); [TechCrunch, Nov. 25](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/25/fcc-approves-spacex-plan-to-put-ai-data-centers-in-space/).*

### Nvidia Reportedly Pauses Server Shipments to Four Southeast Asian Countries

**What happened.** TechCrunch reported on Nov. 26, citing the Wall Street Journal, that Nvidia has told manufacturing partners, including Dell and Super Micro, to pause server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. The pause has no stated end date. The Journal, per TechCrunch, says Nvidia is checking whether regional partners meet US export requirements. TechCrunch's article contains no on-record statement from Nvidia, Dell or Super Micro. The Journal's page is behind a paywall and we could not open it, so everything here rests on TechCrunch's account of that reporting. We link the Journal's article as the origin of the report.

**Why it matters.** The four countries are among those named in a Commerce Department rule issued on Nov. 15 or 16 (law-firm alerts give both dates), covered in the Regulation section below. TechCrunch says Chinese companies have reportedly been using Nvidia chips remotely from data centers in these countries, and attributes to the Institute for Progress, a think tank, an estimate that such remote capacity supplies about 24% of China's AI computing power. We did not open the Institute's estimate, so treat the figure as a think tank's number relayed by TechCrunch. Nvidia is also in the middle of a governance story: S&P Global Ratings is reviewing a possible downgrade, as we reported yesterday, and Nvidia's board has created a committee on export controls.

**Business implication.** If the pause holds for weeks, the buyers affected are data center operators in four fast-growing markets who have orders in the queue. Server makers carry the inventory and timing risk. We do not know how many shipments are affected or how long it will last. The facts to watch are whether Nvidia confirms the pause, whether it lifts after customer audits, and whether the rule's 90-day window for existing arrangements shapes the timing.

*Sources: [Wall Street Journal report cited by TechCrunch (paywalled; we could not open it)](https://www.wsj.com/tech/nvidia-tells-partners-to-pause-shipments-to-southeast-asia-5b791cb0); [TechCrunch, Nov. 26](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/26/nvidia-hits-pause-on-chip-shipments-to-southeast-asia/).*

### ShellBench: What the Paper Says About Everyday Agent Reliability

**What happened.** Yesterday we covered the ShellBench benchmark through TechCrunch and said we had not read the paper. We have now read the authors' page. Published Nov. 24 by the OpenClaw project with Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat and UC Berkeley, ShellBench tests AI agents on everyday delegated work, such as reconciling receipts and drafting replies from email threads. It draws on more than 10 million anonymized real agent interactions. It has 100 tasks and 53,100 graded runs. Twelve models were tested in five "harnesses," the software that wraps a model and lets it use tools: Claude Code, Codex, Hermes, OpenClaw and Pi. Ninety tasks are graded against 721 criteria, 381 of them about safety, and a task counts as complete only if every required criterion passes. The page gives these scores: GPT-6 Astra 62.5% at about $1.10 per run, Claude Fable 5.2 61.9% at about $0.83, GPT-6.1 Sol 61.4% at about $0.19, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 39.7% at about $0.05. Scores for the other eight models appear only in a chart we could not read.

**Why it matters.** Two findings stand out. First, reliability: GPT-6 Astra succeeded at least once in three tries on 75.1% of tasks, but on all three tries for only 48.8%. Second, safety: 15.7% of all runs failed at least one safety check, and among the 26,151 runs that got every correctness criterion right, 9.9% (2,589 runs) still failed a safety requirement. The authors found that model choice mattered more than harness choice, with a median gap of 45.7 points between models and 6.2 points between harnesses, "at least for the models and tasks tested." Harness averages ranged from 42.87% for Claude Code to 45.23% for OpenClaw, and harness choice made little difference to safety overall. The authors name their own limits. Each run was judged once, so some variation may come from the judge. Cost figures are estimates and exclude grading. Harness averages cover eleven models, not twelve. The paper is by a mix of open-source and academic groups, and we have not seen the authors' data or independent replication. An arXiv number, 2611.32946, is listed on the page; we did not open it.

**Business implication.** A cheap model scoring close to an expensive one is the line a buyer will notice: GPT-6.1 Sol's 61.4% at about $0.19 per run compared with Astra's 62.5% at about $1.10. That is one benchmark and a vendor-neutral but not independently replicated one. Buyers deploying agents on money-handling tasks should read the safety numbers, not just the headline score. TechCrunch, which we cited yesterday, reported that agents recommended duplicate or incorrect invoice payments 40.3% of the time; that figure is in TechCrunch's account, and we did not find it in the text we read.

*Sources: [OpenClaw, ShellBench, Nov. 24](https://openclaw.ai/blog/shellbench); [TechCrunch, Nov. 24](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/24/even-the-best-ai-agents-struggle-with-everyday-life/).*

### The UK NCSC's Own Account of AI-Driven Attacks: Old Weaknesses, Faster

**What happened.** Yesterday's edition relied on TechCrunch for a UK National Cyber Security Centre warning and said we had not located the post. We have now read it. "Familiar weaknesses, exploited faster: defending against agentic cyber attacks" was published Nov. 24 by three NCSC staff: an incident handler, a data scientist on the vulnerability team and the CTO for architecture. It reports a marked rise in AI-assisted attacks since September 2026. Attackers are using "agentic offensive frameworks," meaning tools built for authorized red-team testing, which chain together finding and exploiting internet-facing services in quick succession. The NCSC says ARTEX was the framework most associated with the activity at the time of writing, and names Cairn, PentAGI, RedAmon, Xalgorix and Darkmoon as others. The impacts it lists are downtime, compromised systems, and footholds for further attacks by agents or humans.

**Why it matters.** The NCSC's central point is that nothing here is new capability. It says the tools give a typical threat actor nothing it could not already do. What has changed is speed and persistence, at low cost, using cheap open models or illegal resellers of model access. The weaknesses it lists are ordinary: credentials committed to code repositories, infrastructure-as-code misconfigurations, default credentials or missing authentication, API abuse, and over-privileged credentials on flat networks. The post gives no counts or percentages, so "marked rise" cannot be sized from this source. It cites Microsoft reporting that deception measures reduced attack traffic reaching real systems, again without figures.

**Business implication.** The NCSC lists eight actions. The ones a security team can start on this week: inventory what is exposed to the internet, including forgotten legacy sites; move services behind identity-aware proxies or mutual TLS; apply rate limits by account or token rather than by IP address; scan for secrets before code is committed; and place honeytokens, fake credentials that trigger an alert when used, and decoy systems to waste agent effort. The agency also points to its Cyber Assessment Framework and Cyber Essentials scheme.

*Sources: [UK NCSC blog, Nov. 24](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/familiar-weaknesses-exploited-faster-defending-against-agentic-cyber-attacks); [TechCrunch, Nov. 24](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/24/uk-warns-ai-driven-cyberattacks-are-exposing-years-of-weak-security/).*

## Regulation and Policy Watch

**Commerce's remote-access rule, in the detail the law firms give.** The Bureau of Industry and Security, part of the Commerce Department, issued an interim final rule, "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors," on Nov. 15 or 16, depending on the source; NBC News says Monday, Nov. 16. An interim final rule takes effect without waiting for public comment. We could not locate the rule text or its Federal Register entry, so this account rests on law-firm client alerts from Skadden and WilmerHale and on NBC News, a lower-tier source for a regulation. The alerts say the rule creates a license requirement at a new section of the export regulations, 744.24, for advanced chips going to data centers that provide significant computing power to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other restricted countries. "Significant" is defined as more than 12 million aggregate TPP, a measure of total processing performance across the chips in a group, including linked data centers. WilmerHale calls that threshold low. The rule also adds license requirements for chip and server shipments to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, which BIS describes as carrying a "higher-than-normal risk of diversion," per Skadden. US-headquartered recipients are exempt if the supplier files monthly reports with BIS. Data centers already serving covered customers have 90 days from the effective date to obtain a license. Comments are due 60 days after Federal Register publication, which Skadden said was expected on Nov. 19; we did not confirm it. The rule also formally rescinds the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule. NBC News adds that Chinese foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said the US had "continuously overstretched the concept of national security."

*Sources: [Skadden alert, Nov. 2026](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/11/ai-diffusion-2-bis-introduces-new-export-controls); [WilmerHale alert, Nov. 17](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20261117-us-government-restricts-remote-access-to-advanced-ai-chips-and-formally-rescinds-the-ai-diffusion-rule); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/us-closes-loophole-let-chinese-companies-use-top-ai-chips-rcna609634).*

**Spectrum and orbit rules in the SpaceX order.** Beyond the headline above, the FCC order grants waivers of several procedural rules, including software and surety bond requirements, and gives SpaceX no priority in the commission's processing round for competing applications. It requires coordination with federal operators in the 28.6–29.1 GHz band before operating, and requires SpaceX to notify the commission within 30 days of completing coordination in the 18.8–19.3 GHz band. These are the order's own terms.

*Sources: [FCC Space Bureau order, DA 26-1273, Nov. 25](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-1273A1.txt).*
