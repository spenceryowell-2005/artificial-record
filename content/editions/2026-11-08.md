## The Executive Read

Sunday is a day for documents, and the documents say more than the commentary does. Anthropic's amended registration statement, which we downloaded from the SEC's site and read, puts hard numbers on a company growing at a pace few firms have matched. It reports $33.2 billion of revenue in the first nine months of 2026, and it also lists about $895 billion of committed spending, nearly all of it for computing capacity. In the same filing, Anthropic says its internal estimates, and its expectations for future models, indicate it "may imminently meet" the threshold its safety policy calls dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI. It also says it has not designated any current model as past that line. That is a company describing, under securities-law liability, how much AI now helps build AI. Elsewhere, older rules are meeting newer tools. Federal prosecutors in Manhattan have charged three men in an alleged $129 million scheme that used AI to impersonate executives and lawyers. Those charges are allegations, not findings. A federal judge in California dismissed, with permission to refile, a direct-infringement claim against Anthropic's chief executive, according to a press account of the hearing. And TechCrunch, relaying New York Times reporting, says a US intelligence memo describes a Megaspeed executive as recruited to spy on Nvidia's chief executive. Nvidia says its dealings with her were "not unusual." None of that is settled. The common thread is that companies are being asked to put claims in writing, and the writing is now getting read.

## Top AI Headlines

### Anthropic's Amended S-1: $33.2 Billion of Revenue, an $895 Billion Compute Bill, and a Self-Acceleration Warning

**What happened.** Anthropic filed Amendment No. 1 to its Form S-1 on November 2. The amendment is on the SEC's site, and we read the text. Revenue was $33.2 billion for the nine months ended September 30, against $2.5 billion a year earlier. The third quarter alone was $17.0 billion. The filing reports an operating loss of $1.26 billion for the nine months and an adjusted operating income of $4.1 billion. "Adjusted" is Anthropic's own measure, which is not an accounting standard. Net loss was $104.0 billion, of which $94.3 billion is a change in the value of convertible notes and similar instruments. The filing attributes that change mainly to the rise in Anthropic's own valuation, which is a bookkeeping charge, not cash spent. Operating cash flow was positive $6.6 billion, and cash and short-term investments were $131.8 billion. On commitments, the filing says Anthropic has signed purchase and lease agreements worth about $895.4 billion in total. About $447.5 billion of that is expected before the end of 2030, and about $69.8 billion falls due in the twelve months after September 30. That total is about 27 times the nine-month revenue. The share count and price range are still blank, and the company has applied to list on Nasdaq under the symbol "ANT."

**Why it matters.** The filing contains the sentence TechCrunch highlighted on November 2. It says internal estimates and expectations for future models, "rather than our current models," indicate Anthropic "may imminently meet" its Responsible Scaling Policy's definition of "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI." It adds: "We have not designated any of our current models as exceeding this threshold." It also says it does not expect to be able to safely use Claude to fully autonomously design and build successive generations of frontier models without meaningful human supervision, and that it is considering updates to its policy to keep humans in control. Elsewhere, Anthropic says Claude is used for experiments, training data, evaluations and code review in building later models, and that the list-price value of its internal Claude use grew more than 250 times between January and September 2026. In April that monthly value first exceeded its monthly personnel expense. Those are company statements in a prospectus. They are not outside measurements. METR, the independent evaluator we covered Saturday, reached a similar direction by a separate route and said Anthropic withheld most of the evidence METR found most useful.

**Business implication.** The numbers show two things. Revenue is concentrated: the filing says no customer was more than 10% of nine-month revenue, but the top five were about 25%, and many large customers are not on long-term contracts. And the cost side is very large: compute and infrastructure expense was $18.8 billion over nine months, which is 57% of revenue, down from 172% a year earlier. A buyer of Claude should read the customer-concentration and compute-supply risk factors, which warn that cloud partners may not deliver capacity on time. This is informational, not investment advice. An earlier edition said the filing was too large for our tools to open in full. We have now read it, and the figures above are from the filing itself, not from press summaries.

### Megaspeed Executive Was Recruited to Spy on Nvidia's CEO, a Reported US Intelligence Memo Says; Nvidia Disputes the Framing

**What happened.** TechCrunch reported on November 7 that a US intelligence memo claims Alice Huang, an executive at the Singapore-based company Megaspeed, was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security to spy on Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang. TechCrunch attributes the memo's contents to the New York Times, which published the story on November 6, and says the Financial Times reported that the memo contains a further personal allegation about the two. We have not seen the memo, and we are not repeating that second claim. Nvidia told the Times that Jensen Huang's interactions with Alice Huang were "not unusual," and said it does not share classified information with customers, according to TechCrunch. TechCrunch also summarizes a separate Times report from November 7 on New Matrix, a Chinese company that the US Commerce Department authorized to buy advanced Nvidia chips. The Times reporters, per TechCrunch, found the listed office to be "a hollow gray shell," which raised concern that it could be a front for a blacklisted private equity firm.

**Why it matters.** Everything here rests on anonymous sourcing to the Times and the FT. The memo is not published. No official has confirmed it in the coverage we read. Congress is asking questions: as our Saturday edition reported from CNBC, three House committee chairs asked Nvidia's chief executive for a closed-door briefing, proposed for November 18. The Commerce licensing question is separate and arguably simpler. If a licensed buyer's address is a shell, that is a compliance fact that can be checked.

**Business implication.** Chip suppliers that sell through regional cloud operators will face more questions about who the end customer is. Buyers of Nvidia hardware in third countries should expect more documentation requests. This is informational, not investment advice.

### Three Men Charged in Manhattan Over an Alleged $129 Million AI Impersonation Scheme

**What happened.** The US Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York announced charges against Jacob Soall, Sharon Hazan and Guy Sheetrit. Our tools could not load the Justice Department page, so the details below come from press accounts of the November 6 release, including a Jewish News Syndicate report and AP-syndicated local TV copies. Prosecutors allege the three used AI to mimic the voices, and sometimes the images, of corporate executives and law-firm attorneys to persuade finance staff to wire money to accounts they controlled. Reports put the total at more than $129 million, covering 2024 to 2026. Named victims in those accounts include a Missouri hospital system, which lost nearly $4.83 million, and a British financial technology company, which lost about $11.58 million. The charge listed in the release as reported is conspiracy to commit wire fraud, which carries a maximum of 20 years. Soall was arrested in Spain on June 17, Hazan in Spain on August 5, and Sheetrit in the United Kingdom on September 7. Extradition requests are pending. The charges are allegations. The release's own headline uses the phrase "super intelligence."

**Why it matters.** Deepfake fraud, where software clones a real person's voice or face, has been discussed for years. This is a specific indictment with named victims and amounts. Press accounts differ on some details, including whether additional counts were charged, and the loss figure for the Missouri hospital varies slightly by outlet.

**Business implication.** The control that matters is procedural, not technological: a call-back to a known number, or a second approver, for any wire above a set size. A convincing voice on a call is no longer proof of identity.

*Sources: [US Attorney's Office, SDNY, press release (November 6)](https://justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/three-foreign-nationals-arrested-and-charged-scheme-use-super-intelligence-defraud), page not loaded by our tools; [AP via News4Jax (November 6)](https://www.news4jax.com/tech/2026/11/06/3-charged-in-scheme-to-use-ai-to-impersonate-ceos-and-attorneys-to-steal-129-million/); [SecurityWeek (November 6)](https://www.securityweek.com/3-charged-in-scheme-to-use-ai-to-impersonate-ceos-and-attorneys-to-steal-129-million/amp/); [JNS](https://www.jns.org/news/u-s-news/two-israeli-men-among-three-charged-in-129-million-ai-impersonation-scheme).*

### Judge Dismisses, With Leave to Amend, the Direct-Infringement Claim Against Anthropic's CEO in the Music Publishers' Case

**What happened.** According to a trade-press account of the proceeding, which we could not check against the docket, Judge Eumi K. Lee of the US District Court for the Northern District of California on November 4 dismissed the direct copyright infringement claim against Dario Amodei in the music publishers' suit, Concord Music Group v. Anthropic. The claim, titled direct infringement by torrenting, was dismissed with leave to amend, meaning the publishers can refile with more facts. Amodei had asked for dismissal with prejudice, which would have ended it. That account quotes the judge as saying: "Every CEO for any small startup would be liable under the analysis here." It also says the contributory-infringement claim against him was not challenged and remains, that the judge questioned the publishers' list of 20,517 works for resting on "information and belief," and that Anthropic's separate motion on output-based claims and a claim about removed copyright information is still undecided. A joint status report is due November 11.

**Why it matters.** The ruling is narrow. It concerns whether a company's chief executive is personally liable on the pleaded facts. It does not decide whether training on copyrighted material is fair use, and it does not touch the claims against Anthropic itself. Anthropic's S-1 lists the Concord case and other publisher suits among its legal risks, and says a $1.5 billion settlement of the Bartz authors' class action was approved by the court in July 2026.

**Business implication.** For executives of AI companies, personal exposure in copyright suits depends on pleading specific personal acts. For investors reading the S-1, the unresolved claims remain disclosed risks, not resolved ones.

*Sources: [CourtListener docket, Concord Music Group v. Anthropic PBC](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/72199828/concord-music-group-inc-v-anthropic-pbc/), not opened by us; [Courthouse News, "Music publishers suffer setback in copyright case against Anthropic"](https://www.courthousenews.com/music-publishers-suffer-setback-in-copyright-case-against-anthropic/), not opened by us; [Anthropic S-1/A, legal proceedings and risk factors](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026068676/anthropicpbc-sx1ax1.htm). The ruling details are from a secondary trade-press report and should be checked against the docket.*

## Model and Product Updates

**Moonshot AI puts Kimi K3.1 Pro weights on Hugging Face.** The model card describes a mixture-of-experts model (one that activates only part of its parameters for each query) with 2.8 trillion total parameters, 104 billion active per token, a context window of about one million tokens, and text and image input. The benchmark figures are Moonshot's own: 65.7 on Terminal-Bench 4.0, which Moonshot's blog places just behind Claude Opus 5.5 at 66.4 and ahead of GPT-6 Astra at 58.2, and 94.2 on GPQA-Diamond. The license is not a standard open-source one. Offering API access to third parties requires a separate agreement with Moonshot once the licensee's combined revenue passes $20 million over any 12 months. Products with more than 100 million monthly users, or more than $20 million in monthly revenue, must display the model name. Internal use is exempt. *[Hugging Face model card](https://huggingface.co/moonshotai/Kimi-K3.1-Pro); [license](https://huggingface.co/moonshotai/Kimi-K3.1-Pro/blob/main/LICENSE); [Moonshot AI blog](https://www.kimi.ai/blog/kimi-k3-1-pro)*

**Cognition releases SWE-2 Flash, built on Thinking Machines' open model.** Cognition, maker of the Devin coding agent, says the model starts from Inkling-Small (276 billion parameters, 12 billion active) with added mid-training and reinforcement learning. It reports 24.0% on FrontierCode 2.0, up from 3.2% for the base model, and roughly three times the speed of its larger SWE-2, at about one-fifth the price. These are Cognition's own results. The model is free in Devin Desktop and Devin CLI through November 30, and the weights are not public. Cognition says it plans to share them with trusted partners through a program it is still building. *[Cognition blog](https://cognition.com/blog/swe-2-flash)*

**Ricursive releases Ripple-65B Preview.** The company, which says it uses AI for chip design, released a dense 65-billion-parameter model pretrained on 12 trillion tokens, with up to a million tokens of context. It reports 80.52 on TerminalBench 2.1 and compares that with several larger US open-weight models. It also reports a total research-and-development cost of $32 million, which it estimates is about one-tenth of the previous open-weight leader for coding agents. Both claims are the company's own. The page we read does not state the license; check the model card. *[Ricursive, Ripple](https://www.ricursive.com/ripple)*

**Anthropic's filing gives a cost figure for its newest model.** The S-1/A says Opus 5.5 reduced customer cost per task by roughly 40% compared with Opus 5. It is a company claim in a filing, not an independent test. *[SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026068676/anthropicpbc-sx1ax1.htm)*
