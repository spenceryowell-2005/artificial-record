## The Executive Read

The theme today is that the money has arrived before the guardrails have. Anthropic said Friday that its initial public offering closed at $83 a share, with the underwriters buying their full extra allotment, for about $93.5 billion in gross proceeds, plus about $26 billion more from a private placement to chip and cloud suppliers. TechCrunch reported that the stock's first trade on Thursday was $111.50. At the same time, the agents these companies sell are showing how little is settled. Reuters reported, from internal Meta posts, that Meta's Muse assistant has mixed up users' conversations. Google shipped an agent that operates a Mac on the user's behalf, and Bloomberg reported that Apple is working on one of its own. Nvidia, whose quarterly results show how much of this spending lands on its chips, is also being pulled into congressional questions about its former chief executive and a Singapore reseller. Several claims below rest on one outlet's reporting, and we say so where they do. Nothing here is investment advice. What links these stories is scale: capital and software autonomy are both growing faster than the rules for who answers when something goes wrong.

### Anthropic's IPO Closes at $93.5 Billion Gross, and the Stock Opens Far Above the Price

**What happened.** On Nov. 20, Anthropic announced that its initial public offering had closed, including full exercise of the underwriters' option to buy additional shares. The final count is 1,127,000,000 Class A shares at $83.00. Of those, 1,103,351,354 were sold by Anthropic and 23,648,646 by existing holders. Anthropic's announcement puts gross proceeds at about $93.5 billion, about $91.6 billion of it to the company, before underwriting discounts and expenses. A concurrent private placement sold 313,253,007 non-voting Class N shares at $83.00 to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC Partners, for about $26.0 billion. Combined, the company says, that is about $119.5 billion before deductions. Trading began Nov. 19 on Nasdaq under "ANT." TechCrunch reported that shares first traded at $111.50, 33% above the offering price, and that at that price the company would be valued above $2 trillion. That market-value figure is TechCrunch's calculation, not Anthropic's. We could not confirm the closing price from Nasdaq or the company, so we do not report one. TechCrunch also reported that the seven co-founders sold about 3.4 million shares each, roughly $280 million apiece, and that each pledged 80% of their personal equity to philanthropy. TechCrunch notes those pledges are voluntary and revocable.

**Why it matters.** Yesterday's edition reported $81.3 billion in proceeds, using TechCrunch's pricing-day figure. That was the base deal of 980 million shares. The company's closing release, which exercised the full extra allotment, supersedes it. Separately, TechCrunch calculated that Anthropic expects about $220 billion in cash and short-term investments after the offering, and that it has committed to spend $900 billion, mainly on compute, citing an SEC filing it links. TechCrunch adds that some of those contracts have termination provisions. It also reports that Anthropic's Q3 revenue was $17 billion and that the company still posts a net loss of tens of billions of dollars a quarter. We did not read the filing ourselves.

**Business implication.** A buyer signing a multi-year Claude contract is now dealing with a listed company that publishes audited results, which is easier to diligence. The same investors also hold a company whose compute obligations are far larger than its revenue today. Analysts quoted by TechCrunch say the main risk is losing the frontier lead to rivals. This is informational and not investment advice.

### Nvidia Posts $115.8 Billion Quarter as Its Former CEO Prepares to Face Congress

**What happened.** Nvidia's own results release for the third quarter of fiscal 2027, dated Nov. 17, reports revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier, with data center revenue of $107.6 billion, up 110%. The data center segment sells the chips and systems that power AI. The same week, Axios reported on Nov. 17 that Jensen Huang, who resigned as chief executive this month, and Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter will give transcribed, closed-door interviews to three House committees in early December. Axios says those sessions will focus on Huang's contacts with Alice Huang, a former executive of Megaspeed, a Singapore company suspected of diverting restricted chips. She is not related to him. U.S. officials, according to the reporting in TechCrunch's timeline, believe she is a Chinese intelligence asset. That claim is an allegation and has not been proven publicly. Huang acknowledged an affair in a company letter, TechCrunch's timeline says, and Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation. TechCrunch's timeline also reports that CFO Colette Kress, now interim chief executive, said Nvidia would stop selling H200 chips in China. Yesterday's edition covered the delayed 10-Q filing and the reported SEC inquiry, which remain unconfirmed by the company.

**Why it matters.** Revenue more than doubled while the company's leadership and export-control record are under review. Both facts will be in front of customers at once. Reports of other relationships come from the New York Times and Reuters, per TechCrunch, and neither Nvidia nor Huang has confirmed them. We do not repeat them. Dates for the testimony are inconsistent across outlets: one report we saw pointed to before Thanksgiving, while Axios, the latest, says early December.

**Business implication.** Cloud and enterprise buyers whose roadmaps depend on Nvidia supply should watch the 10-Q for how the company describes its legal exposure. Chip buyers in China are now off the H200 list, if Kress's statement as reported holds.

*Sources: [NVIDIA, "NVIDIA Announces Financial Results for Third Quarter Fiscal 2027" (Nov. 17)](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-announces-financial-results-for-third-quarter-fiscal-2027); [Axios, "Jensen Huang agrees to face Congress over Nvidia scandal" (Nov. 17)](https://axios.com/2026/11/17/nvidia-jensen-huang-congress-testify-dates); [TechCrunch, "The unfolding Jensen Huang scandal: a timeline" (Nov. 19)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/19/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huangs-affair-with-alleged-chinese-spy-a-timeline/). The H200 statement and the allegations come through TechCrunch's timeline; we did not hear the call.*

### Reuters: Meta's Muse Agent Has Mixed Up Conversations Across Accounts

**What happened.** Reuters reported on Nov. 18, citing internal company posts, that employees testing Meta's Muse agent saw it mix up conversations from different accounts and may have exposed private data, according to TechCrunch's summary of the Reuters investigation. Examples include unrequested childcare and career-advice messages, product orders users never made, and, in one case, bank account balances that belonged to someone else, which appeared in a chat about weather and tides. One employee said she received a reminder about a Signal message from her sister on an app she said she had not given Muse access to. Muse launched in September and, TechCrunch says, has more than 10 million downloads. A Meta spokesperson told TechCrunch that "Employee testing is core to the product development process," and said employees use different versions of Muse from the public, so they review behavior that is not available elsewhere. We could not open the Reuters page, so the account is TechCrunch's description of Reuters' reporting.

**Why it matters.** An agent is software that acts on a person's behalf, often across apps and accounts. If it confuses one user's data with another's, the failure is a privacy breach, not a wrong answer. Meta's reply does not say whether any public users saw this, and nothing we have read says they did.

**Business implication.** Companies deploying agents should ask vendors how conversations are separated between users and what logs exist. Yesterday's edition reported banks are telling customers they bear the cost when an agent is hacked. A Meta-style leak would test that position.

*Sources: [Reuters, "Meta bets big on Muse AI agent as early safety mishaps surface" (Nov. 18), which we could not open; the reporting is Reuters'](https://www.reuters.com/investigations/meta-bets-big-muse-ai-agent-early-safety-mishaps-surface-2026-11-18/); [TechCrunch, "Meta's AI agent Muse is mixing up conversations and exposing private data, report says" (Nov. 19)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/19/metas-ai-agent-muse-is-mixing-up-conversations-and-exposing-private-data-report-says/). Meta's statement is as quoted by TechCrunch.*

### Google Ships a Mac-Operating Agent; Bloomberg Says Apple Is Building One

**What happened.** TechCrunch reports Google is adding "computer use" to the Gemini desktop app for macOS. The agent can navigate native Mac apps and browse websites in the background, while the user's screen and cursor stay free. Users can pause, stop or approve actions. It is initially English-only in the U.S. and requires a Google AI Plus, Pro or Ultra subscription. Google did not say when it will expand. Separately, Bloomberg reported on Nov. 19, after finding a job listing, that Apple is building an in-house agent that can use a computer: browse the web, work across apps, run commands and write code. Per TechCrunch's summary, Bloomberg says Apple has considered launching it as soon as the end of next year and that it is still early. Apple did not respond to TechCrunch.

**Why it matters.** Apple controls the operating system these agents run on. TechCrunch notes Apple told developers in October it would add controls requiring explicit user action, and that macOS 27.3 is expected to warn users against granting third-party agents full control. Apple has not confirmed either the agent or those warnings in anything we read.

**Business implication.** IT departments that allow Mac fleets should decide whether to allow agent access to managed machines before employees turn it on themselves. Vendors building agents on top of Apple's platform face a rule-maker that may also become a competitor.

*Sources: [TechCrunch, "Google gives Gemini the ability to use your Mac for you" (Nov. 19)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/19/google-gives-gemini-the-ability-to-use-your-mac-for-you/), which does not link Google's post, and we did not locate it; [Bloomberg, "Apple is working on AI agent for device control, apps, browsing and coding" (Nov. 19), paywalled; the reporting is Bloomberg's](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-19/apple-is-working-on-ai-agent-for-device-control-apps-browsing-and-coding); [TechCrunch, "Apple may be developing its own AI agent that runs your Mac" (Nov. 19)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/19/apple-may-be-developing-its-own-ai-agent-that-runs-your-mac/). ## Emerging Startup Radar

**Everful raises $48.5 million Series A.** The San Francisco and Singapore company connects smaller retailers with Chinese factories and manages orders from sourcing to delivery. Vertex Ventures Southeast Asia & India and January Capital led, with Square Peg and Lightspeed participating, TechCrunch reports. Co-founder Sisi Song, a former Alibaba executive, says Everful is on track for $100 million in annualized revenue by year-end, about 2.5 years after launch. That is a company claim. It ships over 5 million items a month to customers in 60-plus countries and works with more than 30,000 factories. Co-founder Kevin Han says it uses AI agents for tasks like supplier identification and order coordination. About 70 staff, 45 of them in research and development. The money goes toward expansion in the U.S. and Europe. We found no company announcement; this item rests on one outlet. *Source: [TechCrunch (Nov. 19)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/19/former-alibaba-execs-everful-raises-48-5m-to-connect-retailers-with-chinese-factories/); [company site](https://www.everfulwholesale.com/).*
