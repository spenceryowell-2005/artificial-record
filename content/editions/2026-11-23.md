## The Executive Read

The thread today is who holds the chokepoints in AI computing, and how fast the people who hold them can be swapped. Nvidia's board put Jensen Huang back in the chief executive's chair less than two weeks after he resigned. It said it had taken input from senior U.S. government officials and was confident his return poses no national security risk. Its own release says an independent investigation continues. The same week, the Commerce Department's chip-export rule reached the Federal Register, and the Nvidia story and the rule are tied together: both turn on whether advanced chips reach Chinese companies through the back door. Amazon, meanwhile, announced it will buy the software that makes models run well on its own Trainium chips. Amazon did not state a price. Bloomberg reported $7.5 billion, as relayed by TechCrunch, and we have not seen Bloomberg's own page. Below the chip layer, OpenAI's chief scientist floated a way for labs to agree on limits, and Sierra, a customer-service software company, said it is raising a round at double its valuation from six months ago. We found no new items for several beats, and we say so below. Nothing here is investment advice.

## Top AI Headlines

### Nvidia's Board Reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO, Citing Input From Senior U.S. Government Officials

**What happened.** On Monday, Nov. 23, Nvidia announced that its board had reinstated Huang as president and chief executive, and as a director, "effective immediately." The release says the vote was unanimous and followed discussions with Huang, after which the board was "satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang's candor and ability to lead NVIDIA." It says the board received input from "senior levels" of the U.S. government and, on that basis, is confident the reinstatement poses no national security risk. The release gives no detail of that consultation. Board chair Harvey C. Jones remains chair. Colette Kress, who served as interim CEO, continues as chief financial officer. According to TechCrunch, Huang had resigned on or around Nov. 12 after admitting at least two extramarital affairs, one with a woman the U.S. government suspects of being a Chinese spy, and Jones had said on Nvidia's third-quarter earnings call that the board did not intend to reinstate him. We read Nvidia's release, not the earlier statements.

**Why it matters.** The release says the board formed a committee of independent directors, advised by independent counsel, after "reports that Mr. Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort," and that this investigation "will proceed to conclusion." It also creates a National Security and Export Control Committee of independent directors and says Nvidia is searching for a Chief Compliance Officer. President Donald Trump praised the decision on Truth Social. TechCrunch reports he said the attorney general had confirmed the government has no evidence of unlawful conduct by Huang, and notes that Attorney General Todd Blanche has made no formal statement and that no Justice Department material was linked. We could not verify the president's claim independently. TechCrunch also reports that Elon Musk, Replit's Amjad Masad, Epic Games' Tim Sweeney and Y Combinator's Garry Tan signed a petition urging reinstatement, and that Nvidia shares opened about 30% higher, trading near $159.95 at 9:51 a.m. ET.

**Business implication.** Customers, suppliers and regulators now deal with a company whose chief executive's status moved twice in under two weeks, with the government's view cited as a reason in each direction. The new committee and compliance hire are the board's answer to the export-control questions. Buyers of Nvidia hardware should watch for the outcome of the independent investigation, which the company has not dated.

*Sources: [NVIDIA, "NVIDIA Board Reinstates Jensen Huang as President, CEO and Board Director" (Nov. 23)](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-board-reinstates-jensen-huang-as-president-ceo-and-board-director); [TechCrunch, Nov. 23](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/23/nvidia-reverses-course-and-brings-jensen-huang-back-as-ceo/); [TechCrunch, Trump's statement, Nov. 23](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/23/trump-endorses-jensen-huangs-return-to-nvidia-says-us-has-no-evidence-of-wrongdoing/). Background on the resignation and share-price moves comes from TechCrunch's reporting; we did not read an Nvidia filing.*

### Amazon Agrees to Buy Decart's Inference Software as It Prepares to Sell Trainium Chips

**What happened.** On Nov. 23 Amazon announced that it will buy the proprietary technology behind the Decart Optimization Stack, or DOS, from Decart, an Israeli startup. Decart's co-founders Dean Leitersdorf, Moshe Shalev and Orian Leitersdorf, plus its researchers and engineers, will join Amazon. Amazon says the team has worked with it since 2025 on Trainium, its AI chip line, and that the deal will strengthen its custom-silicon work and speed up "inference," the stage where a trained model answers requests. Closing depends on customary conditions, including regulatory clearance. Amazon's post does not disclose a price. TechCrunch reports that Bloomberg put the price at $7.5 billion; we could not open Bloomberg's report, so treat the figure as reported, not confirmed.

**Why it matters.** Software that tunes models to a particular chip is a large part of why Nvidia's chips are hard to replace. TechCrunch notes that Decart's stack helps models run on Nvidia, Amazon and Google hardware, and reports that Amazon, which has used Trainium in its own data centers so far, is preparing to sell the chips to customers. Amazon's post does not say that; it is TechCrunch's account. Peter DeSantis, Amazon's senior vice president for foundational models and custom silicon, said, "Every leap forward in AI starts with getting more out of every chip."

**Business implication.** If Amazon does sell Trainium directly, tools that lower the cost of moving a model off Nvidia become part of the competitive pitch. Customers should ask for measured Trainium results on their own workloads. The announcement gives none.

*Sources: [Amazon, "Amazon to purchase Decart's AI optimization technology" (Nov. 23)](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-decart-ai-optimization); [TechCrunch, Nov. 23](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/23/amazon-snaps-up-decarts-ai-optimization-tech-for-7-5b-as-it-prepares-to-sell-trainium-chips/). The $7.5 billion figure is Bloomberg's, via TechCrunch; Amazon did not state a price.*

### The Commerce Department's Remote-Access Chip Rule Is Now in the Federal Register, With Comments Due Jan. 19

**What happened.** We reported the Bureau of Industry and Security's Nov. 16 announcement earlier this week and said we could not confirm dates. The Federal Register record now answers that. The interim final rule, "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors," was published Nov. 19 (91 FR 74970, document 2026-23529), is effective that day, and takes comments until Jan. 19, 2027. It does four things. It adds licence requirements for overseas data centers that remotely supply significant computing power to companies headquartered in Country Group D:5 (the list of countries under U.S. arms embargoes and similar restrictions) or Macau. It requires a licence to ship advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, which the rule describes as higher-than-normal diversion risks. It tightens the presumptions that chip fabricators and packagers face. And it formally rescinds the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule, which the document says was never enforced. U.S.-headquartered companies, and companies with a U.S.-headquartered ultimate parent, are exempt from the five-country requirement if certain reporting conditions are met.

**Why it matters.** The old route for Chinese firms was to rent chips sitting in a third country. This rule makes the data center and the chip exporter responsible for knowing who the customer is. It also pulls five large data-center markets into the licence system. A separate Fortune commentary by three Georgetown CSET analysts argues the rule leaves a gap: U.S.-owned data centers rent capacity to providers that serve Chinese open-weight models, meaning models whose trained settings are published for anyone to run. They say DeepSeek's latest model is offered by at least 16 inference providers based outside China, including Amazon Bedrock. That is the analysts' reading of Artificial Analysis data, not a government finding.

**Business implication.** Data-center operators in the five named countries and any operator serving Chinese-headquartered customers have a compliance task and a comment window. The fine print on thresholds sits in the rule text; we summarise only what the Federal Register record states.

*Sources: [Federal Register, document 2026-23529](https://www.federalregister.gov/d/2026-23529); [BIS press release, Nov. 16](https://bis.gov/press-release/bis-issues-rule-addressing-remote-access-american-super-intelligence-technology); [Fortune commentary by CSET authors, Nov. 21](https://fortune.com/2026/11/21/data-centers-us-chips-serve-chinese-models/). The rule summary is from the Federal Register's record of the document; we did not read the full rule text.*

### OpenAI's Chief Scientist Proposes Labs Agree to "Common Limits," Tied to a Measure Called Perplexity

**What happened.** On Nov. 22 OpenAI published "Perplexity and Intelligence" by Jakub Pachocki, its chief scientist. Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next word-piece in a text; lower is better. Pachocki argues it is the most useful number for tracking progress, and proposes that frontier developers agree not to cross certain perplexity-based thresholds until a concrete monitoring milestone is reached, such as a successor to today's chain-of-thought monitors (tools that read a model's step-by-step reasoning to spot trouble). He says OpenAI tracks the log of perplexity on a fixed held-out sample of its own internal code. By his figures, GPT-3.5 was about 0.99, GPT-4 about 0.91, and below 0.8 came "transformative capabilities" in cybersecurity and mathematics. A model at about 0.76 produced notable results on open math problems OpenAI had posed. He expects more such capabilities in a 0.68 to 0.70 window, without saying what they will be. He also writes that AI contributing to pretraining research could produce a step change, "possibly as early as 2027."

**Why it matters.** This is an OpenAI executive, in OpenAI's own post, proposing a self-restraint mechanism. The post lists its weaknesses. Perplexity does not measure any specific task. Labs use different test data, so cross-lab comparison is hard. Reasoning techniques raise capability without lowering perplexity. TechCrunch reports that Sam Altman called the post "the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while," and notes that Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has published a plan to "pace the frontier." We did not read Amodei's plan, and TechCrunch's statement that Altman and Elon Musk endorsed it is the outlet's characterisation, not something we confirmed.

**Business implication.** Nothing binds anyone yet. Buyers of frontier models and their regulators should watch whether any other lab publishes a comparable number, because without one the proposal cannot be tested.

*Sources: [OpenAI, "Perplexity and Intelligence" (Nov. 22)](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/); [TechCrunch, Nov. 22](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/22/openais-chief-scientist-has-thoughts-on-how-to-limit-ai-development/). The perplexity figures are OpenAI's own and rest on an internal dataset no one outside OpenAI can check.*

## Model and Product Updates

**OpenAI's MedChemBench.** On Nov. 20 OpenAI published a benchmark for medicinal chemistry: 99 problems drawn from 117 papers and patents, covering 761 molecular structures. Models must rebuild a molecule from a description, interpret experimental results, rank compounds and predict numbers. At OpenAI's highest reasoning setting, the company reports GPT-6.2 Astra scored 84.5% of available credit, GPT-6 Astra 83.3%, GPT-6.1 Sol 82.0%, GPT-6.1 Terra 75.1% and GPT-5.6 Sol 58.2%. GPT-6 Astra scored 95.4% on rebuilding structures but about half credit on picking compounds and predicting numbers. OpenAI's own caveats: results do not show models can discover drugs, models may have seen the published sources in training, and each answer was a single attempt without tools. The post is also inconsistent on how many models it tested (eleven in the text, nine in a figure caption). These are OpenAI's scores on OpenAI's benchmark. *Source: [OpenAI, Nov. 20](https://openai.com/index/introducing-medchembench/).*

**Two claimed proofs of the Riemann hypothesis, neither accepted.** The Riemann hypothesis is a 167-year-old question about prime numbers. TechCrunch reports that Levent Alpöge, who works at Anthropic, posted a proof he says he found working with Claude, and that people in a Lean community discussion, including MIT's Andrew Sutherland, say the accompanying Lean code compiles. Lean is software that checks mathematical proofs step by step. TechCrunch adds that a compiling proof shows the logic is valid, not that the formal statement matches the hypothesis, and that no human-readable explanation exists yet. Separately, Stanford's Alessandro Masullo has a draft proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis that Ravi Vakil, president of the American Mathematical Society, posted on his blog on Nov. 20, writing that it is "all human, with no AI." Vakil says Brian Conrad examined it for months and initially found it "looking good," but that several people have reported problems and that every issue must be resolved "to the satisfaction of the community." Neither claim is peer-reviewed. We did not read the papers or the Lean code. *Sources: [TechCrunch, Nov. 20](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/20/ai-claimed-a-solution-to-the-167-year-old-riemann-hypothesis-and-a-human-did-too/); [Ravi Vakil's blog, Nov. 20](https://math216.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/hurried-announcement-alessandro-masullo-grh/).*

Anthropic's newsroom lists its latest post as Nov. 20 (the IPO close), with an undated heading for Nov. 23 and no title. *Source: [Anthropic newsroom](https://www.anthropic.com/news).*

## AI Infrastructure and Market Signals

**Data-center backlash.** In a TechCrunch interview published Nov. 22, journalist Paris Marx, author of *Hyperscale*, says opposition to data centers is bipartisan. He says Microsoft, Amazon and OpenAI have said they will stop using non-disclosure agreements in talks with local governments, and that companies are offering to pay for grid upgrades. He cites reported figures: data centers use more than 20% of Ireland's electricity, and Google's disclosed use was 29% of the water in The Dalles, Oregon. These are Marx's cited claims, and we did not check the underlying sources. *Source: [TechCrunch, Nov. 22](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/22/why-data-centers-are-at-the-center-of-the-big-tech-backlash/).*

TechCrunch's figure of $81.3 billion for the IPO differs from Anthropic's; we use the company's. *Sources: [Anthropic, IPO close (Nov. 20)](https://www.anthropic.com/news/ipo-close); [TechCrunch, Nov. 22](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/22/how-notable-capital-gic-and-baillie-gifford-bet-big-on-anthropic-before-its-ipo/).*
