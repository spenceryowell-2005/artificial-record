## The Executive Read

The week opens with companies being asked to explain themselves. On Sunday, Nvidia's board announced an independent investigation into reports that its chief executive, Jensen Huang, was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. The board says it has no reason to believe national security or Nvidia's secrets were compromised. Huang's own email to staff, quoted by CNBC, says he was unfaithful to his wife. The espionage claims themselves come from reporting by the New York Times and the Financial Times, which Nvidia and Megaspeed, the Singapore cloud company at the centre of it, have disputed. A House briefing has been requested for November 18, and Nvidia reports quarterly results on November 17, according to CNBC. Away from that story, the other pattern of the week is consolidation around coding agents, the AI programs that write and test software on their own. Meta bought Conductor, an app that runs several of them side by side. OpenAI announced a deal for Pierre, a small developer-tools company whose code is already inside Codex, OpenAI's coding agent. Both deals have undisclosed prices. And Thinking Machines launched a model with its weights held back, promising to publish them after safety testing. Weights are the numbers that make up a trained model; publishing them lets anyone run it. The thread is that the people building AI are being asked, by boards, lawmakers and their own safety processes, to show their work before they ship it.

## Top AI Headlines

### Nvidia's Board Opens an Independent Investigation Into Jensen Huang's Dealings With a Megaspeed Executive

**What happened.** Nvidia's board of directors announced on November 8 that a committee of independent directors, advised by outside independent counsel, will oversee an investigation after "recent reports" that Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. Nvidia's statement says the board has "no reason to believe" national security or Nvidia's secrets were compromised, but that the review will consider all available information. It says Huang supports the review and will cooperate, and that the board has confidence in Huang and his leadership team. CNBC reports that Huang sent staff an email on Sunday in which he wrote, "I was unfaithful to my wife." CNBC also quotes: "There is no excuse for what I did." Nvidia says he will keep running the company without interruption.

The woman at the centre is Alice Huang, no relation to Jensen Huang, an executive at Megaspeed. Megaspeed is a Singapore cloud company that rents out computing power and calls itself a core business partner of Nvidia's cloud business in the region, according to CNBC's explainer. The claim that U.S. intelligence officials regard her as a Chinese intelligence asset comes from the New York Times and the Financial Times. CNBC notes that the House Select Committee on China has described her as a "publicly reported Chinese intelligence asset." Megaspeed has said a published article contained "misleading information and incorrect insinuations" and that inspections by U.S. and Malaysian authorities and by Nvidia found no chip-transfer violations, according to CNBC. CNBC could not find a statement from Alice Huang and had asked Megaspeed for comment. Singapore's government said it had not investigated her alleged intelligence ties.

**Why it matters.** Fortune reports that the probe may look at earlier company statements about the relationship, because Nvidia's public line has changed. In October 2025, per Fortune, a spokesman said a 2024 meeting was nothing improper. In October 2026, Huang said on the record that his interactions were "completely normal and not unusual." Fortune says the scope of the board's review is its own reading, not something the board has stated. Nvidia's board has not said what it will examine beyond "all available information." Separately, three House Republican chairs have asked Huang for a closed-door briefing on November 18 about his interactions with Alice Huang and Nvidia's protections against foreign espionage. Reports say he has until November 11 to respond. Nobody has been charged, and the request is voluntary, not a subpoena.

**Business implication.** Nvidia sells the chips most AI companies depend on, so governance at the top matters to customers and to regulators who license its exports. A board-led review is a standard way to show investors that a conflict is being handled. It does not resolve whether any diversion of chips happened. That claim was, according to Nvidia, "thoroughly investigated and debunked last year," per a company spokesperson quoted by CNBC on October 29. Fortune reports Nvidia shares fell 4.24% in overnight trading after the announcement. This is informational, not investment advice.

### Meta Buys Conductor and OpenAI Agrees to Buy Pierre: Two Coding-Tool Deals in One Week

**What happened.** Axios first reported on November 4 that Meta acquired Conductor, a startup whose Mac app runs several coding agents in parallel, each in its own isolated copy of a project. According to The Next Web's account of the deal, Conductor's chief executive, Charlie Holtz, confirmed it on X and on the company's website, and Meta confirmed it to TechCrunch and declined further comment. The price was not disclosed. The Conductor team joins Meta Superintelligence Labs. Conductor stopped new sign-ups on November 4; its cloud service becomes read-only on November 13 and shuts down on November 20, with cloud data deleted afterward; and the desktop app will receive new models and critical fixes until early 2027, per The Next Web's report. Conductor's product page describes an app for running Claude Code, Codex and Cursor agents side by side. Holtz teased "something new" for users early next year. Our tools could not open Axios or Meta's own statement, so the deal terms and wind-down schedule rest on The Next Web's reporting of them.

The same day, OpenAI announced that it will acquire Pierre Computer Company, the maker of Code Storage and open-source libraries called Trees and Diffs. Press coverage says those libraries are already used in Codex and that OpenAI plans to fold Code Storage features into Codex after closing. The deal is subject to customary closing conditions. OpenAI's page blocked our tools; the facts here come from its search-result description and the press. Terms were not disclosed.

**Why it matters.** Both acquirers are buying the layer around the model, not the model itself: the tools that let a developer manage many agents at once and store the code they produce. Meta already has its own coding agent, Muse Code, launched in August, per The Next Web. Conductor was built to work with other companies' agents, including Claude Code and Codex, and Meta has not said whether its 2027 product will keep supporting them. Holtz's comment points to something new, but no details have been published.

**Business implication.** Teams that standardised on a third-party agent-management tool should check export and shutdown dates. Conductor's cloud data will be deleted on November 20. More broadly, small developer-tools companies appear to be more likely to be absorbed than to compete with the labs.

### Thinking Machines Launches Inkling 2 Small With Weights Held Back for Safety Testing

**What happened.** Thinking Machines Lab, the company founded by Mira Murati, introduced Inkling 2 Small on November 5. Its own page describes a mixture-of-experts model, one that activates only part of its parameters for each query, with 594 billion parameters in total and 17 billion active. It accepts image input but not audio. It is available for inference and fine-tuning through the company's Tinker platform, and for now the weights are closed. The company says it intends to release them under the Apache 2.0 open-source license "next week," after external safety testing and red-teaming, meaning deliberate attempts to make the model misbehave. Checkpoints, the saved training states, will be downloadable then.

**Why it matters.** The benchmark numbers are the company's own. It reports a score of 31 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, 86.3% on SWE-Bench Pro v2 (Hard) and 25.4% on FrontierCode v2.0. Its own tables show the model trailing some competitors on several measures and not leading most rows. The company also says Inkling 2 Small still trails closed models such as GPT-6.1 Sol on its alignment evaluations, while scoring better on its FORTRESS risk measure than its earlier Inkling models. It is unusual for a lab to publish a comparison like that on its own launch page. The weights release depends on the safety evaluations finishing; it is a stated plan, not a done deal.

**Business implication.** If the weights ship as promised, companies would be able to host the model themselves without sending data to an outside API. Until then, access is through Tinker only, and pricing is on Tinker's per-token page. The date is the thing to watch: "next week" would fall between November 9 and November 15.

*Sources: [Thinking Machines Lab, "Introducing Inkling 2 Small" (November 5)](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-small/).*

## Model and Product Updates

**Google makes Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash generally available.** Google's announcement on November 5 says the video generation and editing model now makes clips of 3 to 15 seconds in one-second steps, up from a 10-second maximum in Omni 1.1. It supports multi-turn conversational editing and reference inputs: images, motion clips of up to three seconds, and audio cues. Google prices it at $0.07 per second for 720p video, which it says is 30% below Omni 1.1. It is generally available in the Gemini API and Gemini for business, and is in Google AI Studio, Flow, Google Vids and Google Ads. The Gemini app is rolling out to AI Ultra subscribers. Outputs carry an imperceptible SynthID watermark, Google's tag for AI-made media. Google lists fast sports action, rapid script changes and complex physics as still being worked on. All claims here are Google's. *[Google, Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/introducing-gemini-omni-1-2-flash/)*
