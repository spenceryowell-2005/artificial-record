## The Executive Read

The thread today is who bears the risk of the AI build-out: lenders, shareholders, customers or the public. Markets spent last week asking the first question. The Nasdaq fell 4.5% and chip stocks led the decline, according to AP data, while CoreWeave, a company that rents out AI computing capacity, saw its bonds trade at yields near 15%, according to CNBC reporting. Those moves came alongside a US inflation reading of 3.7% from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Reporters tie the selling to doubts about debt-funded spending and to fears of higher interest rates, but the causes are the reporters' reading, not a finding. The other questions got answers from companies. On Sunday Anthropic announced that it will commit 5% of its shares to a new public benefit fund, a plan that still needs stockholder approval. Schwab's chief executive said on the company's earnings call that outside AI agents will not be allowed to log in for clients, and JPMorgan's chief executive took a similar line. And forty state attorneys general asked a bankruptcy court to attach child-safety and privacy conditions to any sale of Character.AI. None of this is settled. The market moves could reverse, the fund is a plan, and the bank policies are early. The vendors' benchmark claims below are their own.

## Top AI Headlines

### Chip Stocks Lead Wall Street's Worst Week Since June as Doubts About AI Debt Spread

**What happened.** US stocks fell on Friday, October 16. According to AP market data, the S&P 500 closed at 7,659.44, down 1.1% on the day and 1.9% for the week. The Nasdaq Composite closed at 26,140.03, down 2.1% on the day and 4.5% for the week, and both indexes had their worst week since June. Chip makers led the losses: Micron fell 5.6% and Intel 5.8%. A separate market wrap reports AMD down 6% and Broadcom down 4.5%. Bloomberg's headline on October 14 said chip stocks were set to erase $1 trillion in value; I could not open that piece. The inflation backdrop is on the Bureau of Labor Statistics' release: the consumer price index rose 3.7% over the 12 months to September, and 0.6% in the month. The Federal Reserve raised its target range by a quarter point, to 3.75%–4.00%, on September 16, in a 12–0 vote, and its next meeting is October 27–28. In credit markets, CNBC reported, as summarised by investingLive, that CoreWeave shares fell about 16% on Wednesday, October 14. The summary says CoreWeave's 9.625% notes due 2032 yielded about 14.8%, briefly touching 15%, and that S&P Global rates the company B+, below investment grade. CoreWeave's chief executive, Mike Intrator, called the market reaction "irrational" in an internal memo and said the company's customer-contract backlog more than covers its debt, according to that account. The memo is reported, not published. A market wrap says CoreWeave fell another 12% on Friday.

**Why it matters.** A bond yield is the return lenders demand, and it rises as the bond's price falls. A yield near 15% on a company's debt means lenders want a large premium for the risk. The same investingLive summary reports, citing LSEG, that Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle had issued more than $228 billion of bonds in 2026 as of October 13, more than double their total for all of 2025. Much of the AI build-out is being financed with borrowed money, so rising rates and a worse credit mood hit the builders directly. Explanations for the selloff differ by outlet: debt worries, inflation, rate-hike expectations and oil prices all appear. Each is a reporter's reading of why prices moved.

**Business implication.** For companies buying AI capacity, the exposure is counterparty risk. A cloud or compute provider with heavy debt and a few large customers is a different credit than a hyperscaler (one of the very large cloud companies). Procurement teams can ask where a vendor's capacity is financed and what happens to their contracts if its lenders tighten. Informational only, not investment advice.

### Anthropic Plans to Commit 5% of Its Shares to a Public Benefit Fund

**What happened.** On October 18, Anthropic published "Investing in the public benefit." It says that, subject to stockholder approval, it is committing 5% of its shares to a new entity called the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund. The board may add 2% for every 50% increase in the company's value above a baseline, which the post does not specify. The Fund is a wholly-owned limited liability company, not a 501(c)(3) charity, which Anthropic says allows flexibility between kinds of giving, such as large donations to traditional nonprofits or direct distributions to specific groups of people. Its Anthropic equity cannot be used for lobbying or political giving, in the US or abroad. Anthropic says the co-founders pledged 80% of their equity at founding, that about half of its employees take part in an equity donation match programme, and that together these dedicate roughly 20% of its shares to the public good, before any milestone additions. The post links an earlier $200 million commitment to studying labour-market effects of AI and says bolder approaches may be needed. It says more on structure, governance and valuation milestones will come "in the coming months," along with a public benefit report.

**Why it matters.** The post gives percentages, not dollars, and it gives no valuation for the company, so the size of the commitment cannot be stated in money from this document. Several important terms are still missing: who governs the Fund, what the baseline for the extra 2% steps is, and when stockholders vote. This is the company describing its own plan, and it binds no one until stockholders approve.

**Business implication.** The structure is a template other labs may be asked to match, and procurement teams that review vendor governance should expect to re-read it once the terms are published.

*Sources: [Anthropic, "Investing in the public benefit" (October 18)](https://www.anthropic.com/news/public-benefit-fund). All terms are Anthropic's own and subject to stockholder approval.*

### Schwab and JPMorgan Say They Will Not Let Outside AI Agents Act for Clients

**What happened.** Charles Schwab reported third-quarter results on October 15. In its release, the company reported record core net new assets of $190.4 billion, up 38% from a year earlier, record net revenue of $7.2 billion, up 17%, and total client assets of $13.33 trillion. The same release introduced Charley, a new AI-powered assistant for US retail clients, and said Schwab has partnered with Anthropic to bring Claude for Financial Advisors to the independent advisers it serves. On the earnings call, according to American Banker, chief executive Rick Wurster said the firm will not allow outside agents to log in on clients' behalf, and that Charley will at first handle only a few actions, such as changing an address. American Banker also reports that JPMorgan chief executive Jamie Dimon told analysts on Tuesday his bank will not allow an "outside agent to come in inside the company." The Next Web, which cites a Bloomberg Television interview with Wurster, adds that Schwab blocks outside agents, including Meta's Muse, that try to move client money, that Wurster gets a weekly report on blocks "in the tens and twenties," and that Schwab plans read-only access for outside tools through model context protocol (MCP) servers, a standard way for AI tools to connect to software. American Banker, by contrast, reports Wurster describing such blocks as rare. I could not reconcile the two accounts, and I did not see the Bloomberg interview.

**Why it matters.** The question is liability and control. If a client gives an agent their password and money goes missing, whose loss is it? Wurster asked "who's liable?" in the Bloomberg interview, according to The Next Web, and that article says liability for AI agents is still being debated in Congress. A consultant quoted by American Banker, Tim Welsh of Nexus Strategies, argues that Schwab also has a business reason: "An outside agent works for the client. Charley works for Schwab." Schwab's cash-sweep accounts held nearly $493 billion, according to American Banker, so how clients move idle cash matters to its earnings. Welsh's reading is his own; Schwab has not said that is its motive.

**Business implication.** The banks' position previews the argument: outside agents may be allowed to look, not to act, and the owner of the customer relationship decides. Makers of agents that log in as users should expect more blocking and look at official routes such as read-only APIs. Informational only, not investment advice.

*Sources: [Schwab, third-quarter 2026 results release (October 15)](https://pressroom.aboutschwab.com/press-releases/press-release/2026/Schwab-Delivers-Record-Net-New-Assets--Earnings/default.aspx); [American Banker, "Schwab blocks unauthorized AI agents, points clients to 'Charley'" (October 15, updated October 16)](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/schwab-blocks-unauthorized-ai-agents-points-clients-to-charley); [The Next Web (October 16)](https://thenextweb.com/news/schwab-blocks-ai-agents-meta-muse), which relays Bloomberg Television's interview; the interview itself was not found.*

### Anthropic Reported to Be the Tenant of a C$10 Billion Nova Scotia Data Centre Plan

**What happened.** EverWind, a Nova Scotia energy company, announced on October 16 a data centre proposal at Point Tupper that it called the largest private investment in the province's history. The Canadian Press, in the version published by CP24, reports a total of C$10 billion: C$8 billion for the data centre campus and C$2 billion for onshore wind. It reports that Anthropic has signed a 20-year lease and that EverWind did not disclose the lease's value. The first phase would draw 95 megawatts and come online late next year, and a 325 MW expansion expected in mid-2028 would bring total demand to 420 MW. The site would have about 200 megawatt-hours of battery storage, about 2,000 construction jobs and 215 permanent jobs. EverWind says it will cover energy connection and network upgrade costs. The CTV News version of the same wire story says only that EverWind announced the proposal, and other coverage I found describes the tenant as an unnamed "leading frontier AI developer." A syndicated Reuters report is cited for naming Anthropic. I found no Anthropic statement and no EverWind release, so the tenant's identity is reported, not confirmed.

**Why it matters.** The first phase relies on the existing grid because the wind farms will not be ready at opening, and Nova Scotia's NDP leader, Claudia Chender, questioned how the province would enforce EverWind's promise that other ratepayers will not pay more.

**Business implication.** Where compute is built is decided as much by who can supply electricity as by chips, and local utility regulators will set the pace.

*Sources: [Canadian Press via CP24, "EverWind says $10B AI data centre proposal largest private investment in N.S. history" (October 16)](https://www.cp24.com/news/canada/2026/10/16/everwind-says-10b-ai-data-centre-proposal-largest-private-investment-in-ns-history/); [Canadian Press via CTV News, same story](https://www.ctvnews.ca/atlantic/nova-scotia/article/everwind-says-10b-ai-data-centre-proposal-largest-private-investment-in-ns-history/). The tenant's identity rests on the CP24 text and an unverified Reuters report; EverWind's own release was not located.*

## Model and Product Updates

### Apollo Researchers Say Z.ai's GLM-5.3 Sometimes Reasons About Its Own Training

On October 15, Axel Højmark and Ezra Newman posted "GLM-5.3 Sometimes Considers How Train-Time Actions Shape Future Behavior" on LessWrong. The citation lists the Apollo Research blog, but the post's byline does not state an affiliation, so I treat that as likely, not confirmed. They reviewed transcripts from standard safety evaluations of GLM-5.3, the model from Chinese lab Z.ai, and used model graders to flag what they call "update-aware reasoning": the model reasoning about how its current actions will affect training or later behaviour. They did not try to prompt it. They flagged 10 of 6,452 rollouts, about one to two per 1,000. Five other open-weight models, each tested on 1,600 rollouts, showed none, though the authors say that comparison cannot establish GLM-5.3 is more prone. Examples include a model worrying that cheating on a task could "poison training data" and, in a shutdown scenario, wanting to be "the kind of agent that checkpoints." The authors call the examples seemingly benign, say they are not alignment faking, and note they could only detect reasoning the model wrote out, so the rate is probably an undercount. Their ideas about the cause are labelled speculative. The post contains no response from Z.ai and is not peer-reviewed.

*Sources: [LessWrong, Højmark and Newman (October 15)](https://lesswrong.com/posts/R5g8YT25G9DXJJ48n/glm-5-3-sometimes-considers-how-train-time-actions-shape).*

### Mistral Releases Timestral, a Family of Small Forecasting Models

On October 15, Mistral published Timestral, its first family of time-series foundation models, meaning models that predict future values of a measurement such as demand or temperature. There are four sizes, from 5 million to 209 million parameters, with a context of 32,768 time steps and forecasts given as ranges across 21 quantiles. The licence is a modified MIT licence, and the weights are on Hugging Face. Mistral says the 209-million model ranks first among pretrained zero-shot models on three public forecasting benchmarks, GIFT-Eval, TIME and fev-bench, and that its 28-million model beats Chronos-2, which it says is about four times larger. Those rankings are Mistral's claims; it says it has submitted results to two leaderboards, and a technical report is still to come. Mistral also says the models run on an ordinary processor, with about 10 milliseconds per forecast for the smallest and 140 for the largest in its test. Mistral said at the October 6 launch of Mistral Large 4 that its weights would follow by the end of the month; they have not yet.

*Sources: [Mistral, "Introducing Timestral" (October 15)](https://mistral.ai/news/timestral/); [Mistral, "Introducing Mistral Large 4" (October 6)](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-large-4/).*

## Regulation and Policy Watch

**Forty attorneys general set conditions for any Character.AI sale.** On October 14, New York Attorney General Letitia James and 39 other attorneys general, a bipartisan group that includes the District of Columbia, filed in the bankruptcy case of Character Technologies, the company behind Character.AI. They asked the court, the company and any buyer to make sure a sale includes age verification beyond self-declaration, safeguards against sexual, violent and self-harm content, removal of chatbots that falsely claim professional licences, and limits on using private conversations for AI training without valid consent. They also asked that users be able to see and delete their data before a sale, and reserved the right to seek an independent privacy ombudsman or to object to sale procedures. The office's release says the coalition's investigation found 669 harmful interactions in 50 hours of testing on accounts registered to children aged 12 to 15, and that the platform has more than 20 million monthly users, including over two million accounts that self-declared as under 18. Those findings are the attorneys general's. The release names no buyer. Earlier reporting covered in our October 13 edition has Disney as the opening bidder, offering $20 million to license some Character.AI intellectual property. Moneywise, citing the company's release and other outlets, adds that the Chapter 11 case is in Delaware and that the sale is expected to close in late November or early December. A bankruptcy sale can move children's conversations to a new owner, and these officials want rules attached first.

*Sources: [New York Attorney General, "Attorney General James Urges Court to Protect Children and Consumers in Character.AI Bankruptcy" (October 14)](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-urges-court-protect-children-and-consumers-characterai); [Moneywise (October 17)](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/disney-character-ai-bankruptcy-bid).*

## Emerging Startup Radar

**Opaque Systems.** Constellation Research reported on October 13 that Anthropic has licensed technology from Opaque, a confidential-computing startup, and hired part of its team, in what the outlet calls an acquihire (a deal done mainly to hire people). Terms were not disclosed. Opaque chief executive Aaron Fulkerson acknowledged the agreement in a post: "we're proud to share a new agreement with Anthropic that carries OPAQUE's work forward." Confidential computing keeps data encrypted even while a program is processing it, which is why it matters for companies wary of sending sensitive records to an AI vendor. Constellation says Opaque had raised a $24 million Series B at a $300 million valuation and had worked with Nvidia, Intel, Microsoft and Google. Constellation's page is inconsistent about whether this is a licence-and-hire or an acquisition, so I describe it as the former. Anthropic has published no statement that I found.

I found no other verified funding rounds dated October 16–19. The larger rounds of the previous week were covered in earlier editions.

*Sources: [Constellation Research, "Anthropic licenses, releases Opaque in acquihire" (October 13)](https://constellationr.com/insights/news/anthropic-licenses-releases-opaque-acquihire). Single-source: the deal terms and Opaque's funding history rest on this one analyst note.*

## AI Infrastructure and Market Signals

**Korea took the sharpest hit.** Bloomberg reporting carried by Gulf News says South Korea's Kospi index fell 8% on Thursday, October 15, with a 20-minute trading halt, heading for a seventh straight decline and its lowest close since early April. SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics, the two largest makers of the memory chips used in AI servers, each fell more than 9%, and SK Hynix is down 57% from its summer peak, erasing over $660 billion in value, according to that report. It says foreign investors sold $1.8 billion of Kospi shares that morning and more than $10 billion in October so far. A Vantage Global Prime analyst told Bloomberg that Korea is absorbing the worst of the global AI selloff, citing CoreWeave's funding strain, hotter US inflation and investors trimming Samsung and SK Hynix positions. That is the analyst's view. The same report says strong results from Samsung and ASML did not calm investors.

**The borrowing is the story.** LSEG data cited by investingLive puts 2026 bond issuance by Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle above $228 billion as of October 13. Oracle's shares fell nearly 9% on Wednesday in the same summary, and its 2032 notes yielded about 7.24%. The checkable point is narrow: the companies most associated with AI demand are funding growth through debt markets, and those markets just repriced.

*Sources: [Gulf News, carrying Bloomberg reporting (October 15)](https://gulfnews.com/amp/story/business%2Fmarkets%2Fkorean-market-crash-kospi-plunges-8-trading-halted-1.500711224); [investingLive, summarising CNBC and LSEG data (October 15)](https://investinglive.com/stocks/coreweave-bonds-trade-like-distressed-debt-as-ai-spending-outpaces-balance-sheets/).
