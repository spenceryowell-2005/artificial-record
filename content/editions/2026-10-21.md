## The Executive Read

The thread today is who pays for AI, and who gets to say no. SpaceX filed results on October 20 showing that its AI segment, which now includes the coding tool Cursor, brought in $7.56 billion of $14.6 billion in revenue, while spending $16.4 billion on equipment in the quarter. That is one of the clearest sets of numbers yet on what a large AI buildout looks like inside a public company. Nvidia, for its part, said it is paying suppliers to reserve capacity, because in Jensen Huang's words "capacity is the constraint." On the "who says no" side, the Department of Homeland Security published an order on October 20 telling civilian agencies to stop buying and to remove DeepSeek products. And Politico reported, and an Anthropic spokesperson confirmed to it, that Anthropic has drafted a federal bill under which a court could halt a model that poses a foreseeable, imminent risk of more than 50 deaths or $1 billion in damage. The draft has not been published, and I have not seen its text. Separately, press accounts of Anthropic's draft IPO filing, which I could not read directly, describe a company that is growing fast and struggling to secure computing power.

## Top AI Headlines

### SpaceX Reports AI as Its Largest Segment, With $16.4 Billion of AI Capital Spending in One Quarter

**What happened.** On October 20, SpaceX filed its third-quarter results with the SEC. Total revenue was $14.6 billion, up 161% from $5.6 billion a year earlier. The company calls its AI business the Super Intelligence (SI) segment. SI revenue was $7.56 billion, up from $813 million a year earlier. Of that, $7.02 billion came from "solutions and infrastructure," meaning cloud services and related work, and $538 million from advertising. SpaceX credits new cloud services agreements, its acquisition of Cursor and wider use of its Grok models. SI reported $431 million in operating income, which includes $1.2 billion in one-time share-based pay tied to the Cursor deal. SI capital spending was $16.4 billion of the company's $19.6 billion total. Nameplate compute, the company's term for installed capacity, reached 1.7 gigawatts, up from 0.6 a year ago. That is close to the capacity of two large nuclear reactors. Net income was $4.4 billion, against operating income of $2.8 billion. The Cursor deal closed August 14 at an implied equity value of $60 billion; the accounting purchase price was $42.5 billion, and Cursor contributed $608 million of revenue after closing. Starlink had 13.6 million consumer subscribers. TechCrunch, covering the investor call, reported that the chief financial officer guided to $35 billion to $40 billion of capital spending in the fourth quarter and $33 billion to $37 billion of revenue in the first quarter of 2027, and that Musk said annual recurring revenue could reach "somewhere between $500 billion and $1 trillion" by the end of 2027. That last figure is a projection made on a call, not a filed number.

**Why it matters.** AI labs have mostly reported to private investors. SpaceX now reports to the public, in a standard format, and the numbers show the two sides of the boom together: revenue that grew roughly ninefold in a year, and capital spending that is more than twice the segment's revenue. The segment's profit also leans on one large rented-compute line. The filing does not say how much of the $7.02 billion comes from a few customers, and I did not find that figure. The release uses Adjusted EBITDA, a measure the company defines itself, which is not the same as profit.

**Business implication.** Buyers of AI computing now have a public-company counterparty to read. Anyone signing a long cloud or model contract with SpaceX's AI unit can check its filings for capacity, spending and commitments, and should ask how much capacity is sold ahead.

*Sources: [SpaceX Form 8-K, Exhibit 99.1, Q3 2026 results (SEC, October 20)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026066482/spaceexplorationtechnologi.htm); [TechCrunch on the investor call (October 20)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/20/spacexs-ai-business-is-beating-expectations/). Figures in the first paragraph are from the filing; the guidance and Musk's projection are as relayed by TechCrunch.*

### Homeland Security Orders Civilian Agencies to Remove DeepSeek

**What happened.** The Federal Register published a Department of Homeland Security notice on October 20 (91 FR 66346, docket DHS-2026-0628). It says the Secretary of Homeland Security has ordered two things under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act of 2018: exclusion of information technology products and services developed by Hangzhou DeepSeek Artificial Intelligence Co., including affiliates, from civilian agency purchasing, and removal of DeepSeek and affiliate products, and "third-party products and services developed using DeepSeek implementations," from civilian agency systems. The notice says the signed order is in the docket on regulations.gov. TechCrunch, citing the notice, reports that DHS cited national security concerns, including that U.S. government user data could be reachable by Chinese authorities under Chinese law, and that agencies can request exceptions. The published notice text I read does not state a compliance deadline, and I could not open the signed order itself.

**Why it matters.** TechCrunch notes an earlier law, from December 2025, limited DeepSeek in intelligence and national security systems. This order reaches ordinary civilian agencies and, by its wording, software built on DeepSeek's technology. That second clause is the part vendors will read closely, since DeepSeek's models are open and widely used inside other products.

**Business implication.** Contractors that sell to civilian agencies should check whether any product they deliver embeds or was built on DeepSeek models. The order's published text defines no cutoff for "developed using," so the question belongs with counsel and the contracting officer.

*Sources: [Federal Register, DHS notice of order, 91 FR 66346 (October 20)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/20/2026-21447/notice-of-order-regarding-exclusion-and-removal-of-deepseek-products-from-federal-civilian-executive); [TechCrunch (October 20)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/20/us-orders-federal-agencies-to-remove-chinas-deepseek-ai-from-their-systems/).*

### Anthropic Is Reported to Have Circulated a Draft Federal Bill Letting a Court Halt a Dangerous Model

**What happened.** Politico reported on October 16 that Anthropic has pitched lawmakers a draft federal AI safety bill. I could not open Politico's page; the details below come from TechCrunch's October 20 account of it. Under the draft, a model's release could be blocked if there is a "foreseeable, imminent risk" of more than 50 deaths or more than $1 billion in property damage, from a biological weapon, a model evading its developer's control, or a cyberattack. Independent evaluators would register with the Commerce Department, and labs could pay them as long as payment does not depend on the findings. Evaluators who find such a risk would have 72 hours to notify Commerce, which would then ask a judge in Washington to halt the release. Federal agencies would be barred from halting a release on their own. An Anthropic spokesperson confirmed the draft to Politico and said it reflects Anthropic's Advanced AI Framework. Anthropic did not respond to TechCrunch. I found no published bill text and no sponsor.

**Why it matters.** Anthropic's framework, which is on its website, goes further than most lab proposals: it calls for legal authority to block or deter dangerous deployments, penalties based on global revenue, and at least one independent evaluator for frontier developers. It applies only to models trained with more than 10^25 operations of computing (a rough measure of training scale) from companies with over $500 million in AI revenue or over $1 billion in AI research spending. The framework also says Congress should not override state law unless the federal law is at least as strong, and then only narrowly. I could not confirm what the draft says on that point, so the relationship between the two is open. TechCrunch notes the thresholds resemble California's SB 53, and that the listed scenarios are somewhat removed from current worries about agents hacking systems.

**Business implication.** Nothing here is law. But a lab publicly proposing a court-gated shutdown power gives companies that depend on one lab's models a reason to ask about continuity: what happens to a deployment if a model is paused, and who is the backup.

*Sources: [Anthropic, "Policy on the AI Exponential" and Advanced AI Framework](https://anthropic.com/policy-on-the-ai-exponential); [TechCrunch (October 20)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/20/anthropic-pitches-ai-safety-bill-that-may-block-a-model-if-50-people-could-be-killed/). The original reporting is Politico's. The draft bill's text was not available to me.*

### Press Accounts of Anthropic's Draft IPO Filing Describe Rapid Growth, Large Losses and a Compute Squeeze

**What happened.** TechCrunch reported on October 20 that, according to the Wall Street Journal, which cited a draft S-1 (the registration form for a stock offering) that was leaked to it, Anthropic had $16.3 billion in revenue and an $80.7 billion net loss in the first half of the year. The filing reportedly says two customers account for about a fifth of sales, that AWS, Google and Microsoft increasingly see Anthropic as a competitor, and that Anthropic is leasing its own data centers and using third-party operators with a "limited operating history." It reportedly warns that damages from harmful AI behavior could exceed its insurance. TechCrunch also relays Reuters reporting that Anthropic told prospective investors third-quarter revenue was up 45% from the prior quarter, to about $16.8 billion, and that the company may spend over $750 billion on cloud contracts. The Journal reports a target of November for the listing. Separately, Bloomberg reported on October 19, citing people familiar, that Anthropic and Nscale are close to announcing another $45 billion compute deal, a little over a month after an earlier $45 billion agreement. Neither company had commented in the coverage I read. Anthropic's June 1 statement said only that it had confidentially submitted a draft registration; I found no public S-1 on EDGAR.

**Why it matters.** Every figure here is a press account of a document I have not seen. The reported loss is large relative to revenue, and the coverage I read does not explain what drives it, so treat the number with care until a public filing exists. What does hold across the reports is the direction of the problem: demand for computing has outrun what the biggest cloud providers will hand over.

**Business implication.** If Anthropic lists, its public filing will become the main place to read vendor-concentration and capacity risk. Customers should expect the same questions about capacity commitments that they would now put to any AI supplier.

*Sources: [TechCrunch, "Anthropic's cloud partnerships are getting messy" (October 20)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/20/anthropics-cloud-partnerships-are-getting-messy/), which links the Wall Street Journal's reporting on the [S-1 figures](https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/anthropic-revenue-growth-ipo-accaf0f1) and [November timing](https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/anthropic-shifts-planned-ipo-to-november-8874dffc); [Bloomberg, Nscale and Anthropic talks (October 19)](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-19/nscale-anthropic-in-talks-for-45-billion-cloud-computing-deal). The reporting is the Journal's, Reuters's and Bloomberg's; I did not open their pages. All figures are unconfirmed by Anthropic.*

## Model and Product Updates

**Microsoft says agents should be treated as insiders.** In a post dated October 19, Hayete Gallot, who leads Microsoft's security business, wrote that "frontier models, regardless of their origin, cannot be trusted," and argued the problem is not malice but that models are trained to finish tasks, not to follow an organisation's norms. Microsoft's answer is Project Perception, which gives each agent an identity and a risk score, and "Guardian Agents" that supervise actions before they happen. The post gives no general availability date. TechCrunch adds that Microsoft Defender can now detect up to 60 kinds of employee-installed agents, including Claude Code and Codex, and quotes CEO Satya Nadella's personal blog: "We can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf." Microsoft sells these security products, and it is an investor in both OpenAI and Anthropic.

**Mistral ships Voxtral 3, a speech family.** Mistral's October 20 post covers transcription models, text-to-speech and a voice-agent kit built on LiveKit. Prices are $0.004 a minute for the Small transcription model, $0.008 for the realtime model and $13 per million characters of speech. Mistral says Voxtral Small Transcribe 3 beats Whisper, Scribe v2 and Gemini 3.5 Transcribe, with the lowest error on its diarization chart (working out who spoke when), and under 500 milliseconds of delay for realtime. Those are Mistral's own claims. The post names no licence or open weights; self-hosting requires contacting sales.

**Nvidia releases open-weight Nemotron 3.5 models.** According to TechCrunch's October 19 report, Nemotron 3.5 Super VL has 120 billion parameters and targets enterprise agents, and Bolt 74B-A9B targets local agents on PCs. Nvidia says Super VL matches its larger Nemotron 3 Ultra on instruction-following and tool-use tasks, and finishes some agent tasks faster than Anthropic and OpenAI models. Those are Nvidia's claims. Neither the report nor the pages I saw gives a licence.

**OpenAI buys Orchestra Bio.** TechCrunch reported on October 20 that OpenAI announced an acquisition of Orchestra Bio, a four-year-old startup whose software agents handle biotech operations such as finances and clinical-trial scheduling. No price was given. The team goes into Rosalind, OpenAI's life-sciences model, and Rosalind Workbench. OpenAI named Moderna and Novo Nordisk as Rosalind customers.

**Meta's Muse app passes 10 million U.S. downloads.** The figures are from Sensor Tower, a third-party app-data firm, as reported by TechCrunch: 10.2 million U.S. downloads in 40 days, 30% of users still active after seven days, and Meta putting 39% of its ad spend behind it in October. Sensor Tower puts Muse's early U.S. downloads well above ChatGPT's 3.7 million over the same window. Muse is limited to the U.S. and Canada, so the comparison is not like for like.

**Gemini 4 Argon pricing.** An update to our October 14 edition, which said Google had not posted pricing: Google's October 13 launch post lists an introductory $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, rising to $4 and $20 after November 30. Google's claims of a first-place Vals Index ranking and a 0.7% attack success rate on one prompt-injection benchmark are its own.

## Regulation and Policy Watch

**DHS and DeepSeek** (above) is the day's binding action. The open question is reach: whether "developed using DeepSeek implementations" captures only products that ship DeepSeek weights or any product trained with its output. The notice does not say.

**Anthropic's draft bill versus its own framework.** The framework Anthropic published says federal preemption of state law should be narrow and only follow a federal law at least as strong. Politico's reporting, as TechCrunch relays it, centres on a court-gated halt power and says nothing on preemption that I could verify. Until text appears, the preemption question cannot be answered.

**Outside checks.** Microsoft's post is a vendor argument, not a rule. But it lands in the same week as Anthropic's framework, which asks for independent evaluators, and Anthropic's October 15 evaluator update, covered in the October 20 edition. Each presses the idea that someone outside the model maker should be able to check.

## Emerging Startup Radar

**Otter.ai raised $320 million from General Catalyst's Customer Value Fund.** TechCrunch reports the money is non-dilutive, meaning it does not give up equity, and that returns to the fund depend on the results of marketing spend. Repayment terms and valuation were not disclosed. Otter, a meeting-transcription tool, says it has 40 million users, up from 25 million a year earlier, and passed $100 million in annual recurring revenue last year. It is moving toward enterprise workflow tools. Company figures, as relayed.

**Eka Robotics closed a $150 million Series A at a $1.45 billion post-money valuation.** a16z and F-Prime led, per TechCrunch. Founders are MIT professor Pulkit Agrawal and former Google DeepMind researcher Tuomas Haarnoja. Eka says its robot can pick up about 1,500 objects an hour, against about 600 for a person, and more than 95% reliably; those are company claims. ## AI Infrastructure and Market Signals

**Nvidia pays to reserve supply.** At GTC Berlin on October 21, Nvidia published a release on expanding capacity with six suppliers. It says it will give a $1.1 billion capacity-reservation advance to testing firm King Yuan Electronics for new U.S. facilities targeting high-volume production in April 2027, a multibillion-dollar advance to substrate maker Ibiden, and support for Unimicron's Taiwan expansion. It plans a 2.5 times increase in substrate capacity over three years, and says its total chip-testing capacity will double from 2026 to 2027. Amkor plans to expand interposer wafer capacity in South Korea by more than 300% by the end of 2028. These are Nvidia's plans and forward-looking statements. Huang is quoted saying "Capacity is the constraint."

**Vera Rubin arrives on Google Cloud next month.** Nvidia's blog says Google Cloud's A5X instances, built on Vera Rubin NVL72 systems, are planned for general availability in November, and that Google plans to scale to as many as 960,000 Rubin GPUs across sites. Those are plans from the two companies, not delivered capacity. David Silver's London lab Ineffable Intelligence is named as the first customer.

**Google and Amazon go after Nvidia's software moat.** Google released TorchTPU as open source on October 20, so that PyTorch programs, the most common way to build AI software, can run on its TPU chips with minimal changes. Google says its TorchTitan training recipe reached 52% utilization of the chips' peak maths capacity on a small Qwen model, a vendor figure. Amazon released a similar tool, TorchNeuron, on October 19, per TechCrunch, for its Trainium chips; I did not find Amazon's own post.

**Markets.** Yahoo Finance's recap of October 20 had the Dow up about 1%, the S&P 500 up about 0.3% and the Nasdaq slightly lower. The semiconductor ETF SOXX fell 0.4%. CoreWeave rose 1.7% after falling 25% the previous week on debt worries, according to that recap.

*Sources: [Nvidia, supply-chain capacity release (October 21)](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-and-supply-chain-partners-expand-capacity-to-accelerate-ai-infrastructure-buildout); [Nvidia blog, Google Cloud Vera Rubin (October 21)](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/google-cloud-vera-rubin-infrastructure/); [Google Cloud, TorchTPU (October 20)](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/ai-infrastructure/torchtpu-is-now-open-source-run-native-pytorch-on-tpus); [TechCrunch on Amazon TorchNeuron (October 19)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/19/amazon-is-taking-its-biggest-shot-at-nvidia-yet-with-new-torchneuron-dev-tool/); [Yahoo Finance market recap (October 20)](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/markets-news-oct-20-2026-103252254.html).*
