## The Executive Read

Two days after the midterms, the AI industry spent Thursday talking about who pays and who gets to build. Brockman told a Wall Street Journal conference audience that enterprise customers now make up most of OpenAI's revenue, and that the company could be ready for an IPO next year. Meta's president, speaking at the same event, said Democratic wins will make data centers "harder" to build, even as Meta gets calls "every day" from companies wanting to lease its computing capacity. Airbnb's results release said it will build its own travel agent and does not expect people to "use one AI agent for everything." Dropbox announced a $350 million purchase of a storage startup, and The Information reported that Palo Alto Networks is in talks to pay about $500 million for a company that secures AI agents. The pattern is that money is moving toward the plumbing around agents: storage, security, retrieval and access controls. OpenAI also released a hacking-focused model to a vetted group, with its main claims coming from the company and from one outside researcher. We could not open OpenAI's own pages today, so those figures are TechCrunch's account of them. Nothing here shows demand slowing. It shows that the businesses selling AI are being asked, by regulators, voters and customers, to prove what they are for.

## Top AI Headlines

### OpenAI Releases a Cybersecurity Model to a Vetted Group

**What happened.** TechCrunch reports that OpenAI has released ChatGPT Cyber, a model built for offensive security research, meaning finding and developing exploits for software flaws. It is available to the top tier of OpenAI's cybersecurity program, called Daybreak Red. Applicants must verify their identity; one researcher told TechCrunch he uploaded a driver's license. OpenAI says "thousands" of security professionals are in the program and that it plans to widen access to organizations of every size, independent researchers and open-source maintainers. TechCrunch also reports, from OpenAI's linked posts, that the company's models scanned the Linux kernel and prepared patches for 948 potential issues, and found 81 "novel" vulnerabilities in Firefox. A separate OpenAI post, per TechCrunch, announces a $1 billion initiative to support people protecting critical infrastructure such as the power grid and water systems. These are OpenAI's own claims; we could not open the posts. The article's name for the model, and the URL of the announcement, differ ("GPT-6 Cyber" appears in the link), and TechCrunch does not explain the difference.

**Why it matters.** Max Justicz, chief technology officer of Charge Robotics, told TechCrunch he used an OpenAI model to find a severe flaw that could let attackers take over Ubuntu servers. The flaw is tracked as CVE-2026-109518, and the Debian Project has issued a patch. TechCrunch reports no known exploitation. He said the search took about two hours and that he spent roughly $5,000 on subscriptions and tokens to produce several exploits. A token is a chunk of text, about three-quarters of a word, and AI services bill by it. Justicz also argued that restricting access may keep bad actors out but leaves defenders at a disadvantage if attackers can use open-weight models with their safety limits stripped. That is his view, not a finding. We could not open the CVE record's detail page, so we have not checked the flaw's description ourselves.

**Business implication.** Security teams now have a vendor-controlled route to the same tools attackers might eventually have. Access decisions, such as who counts as a defender and who is turned away, become a commercial and reputational question for OpenAI.

### Brockman: OpenAI Could Be IPO-Ready in 2027, and Enterprise Is Now Most of Its Revenue

**What happened.** Speaking at a Wall Street Journal technology conference on Wednesday, OpenAI president Greg Brockman was asked whether OpenAI would be ready to go public next year. TechCrunch quotes him: "I think so. Yes." He said the company will list when it judges the timing right. He described the first and second quarters of 2026 as "pretty rough" for enterprise sales, admitted OpenAI "wasn't even in the conversation" on agentic coding (software agents that write and run code), where Anthropic's Claude Code has gained ground, and said activity picked up in the third quarter. According to TechCrunch's account, he said enterprise revenue had doubled, enterprise is now the majority of revenue, up from roughly 30%, and annualized revenue grew 70% quarter over quarter. TechCrunch notes the article does not make clear whether the 70% applies to the whole company or only enterprise. He cited a recent partnership with JPMorgan Chase.

**Why it matters.** These are statements by an executive at a private company, on stage. We have not seen any filing or audited number behind them, and we have not watched the recorded session. The admission about coding is notable because it is a public concession of lost ground by a company that rarely makes them. Anthropic, a competitor, filed its own public registration with the SEC on October 26 and says it plans to list on the Nasdaq under the symbol "ANT," with share count and price not yet set.

**Business implication.** If both leading labs are heading to public markets, their numbers will soon be checked against filings rather than conference remarks. Customers negotiating enterprise contracts now know OpenAI is hungry for them.

*Sources: [TechCrunch, "OpenAI president Greg Brockman says company could be IPO ready in 2027" (November 5)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/05/openai-president-greg-brockman-says-company-could-be-ipo-ready-in-2027/); [recording of the conference session, linked by TechCrunch and not watched by us](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yfnmAsXqvIs); [Anthropic, S-1 announcement (October 26)](https://www.anthropic.com/news/s1-sec).*

### Meta's President: Election Results Make Data Centers Harder to Build

**What happened.** Dina Powell McCormick, Meta's president, spoke at the same Wall Street Journal conference on Wednesday. TechCrunch reports she said Democratic midterm wins would make it "harder" to build data centers, that the industry has "not done as good a job as we needed to explain the benefits," and that the underlying problem is "how people feel about AI in general." She said Meta has operated data centers for about 20 years, has them in 23 states, and has five planned projects of one gigawatt or larger, one of them in Texas. A gigawatt is roughly the output of one large nuclear reactor. She said successful projects have Meta paying for its own electricity, steering tax surpluses to teachers and first responders, keeping the workforce local and winning bipartisan support. She also said Meta gets "calls every day to lease that compute." The article carries no capital spending figures.

**Why it matters.** CNBC's election coverage, citing Ballotpedia, counts 41 local ballot measures in 10 states that restricted data center construction in different ways, with Ohio alone holding 21. According to CNBC, voters opposed data centers in all but one of them. That one, in Harwood, North Dakota, was a vote against adding review of industrial projects. CNBC also reports that Democrat Amy Acton won Ohio's governorship while supporting a "conditional moratorium" on data centers, and that Democrat Gina Hinojosa unseated Texas Governor Greg Abbott after blaming him for the data center buildout.

**Business implication.** A company that says it is being asked daily to rent out its computing power, while also saying new sites are getting harder, is describing scarcity from both sides. Developers should expect to offer binding commitments on power costs and local hiring. This is informational, not investment advice.

*Sources: [TechCrunch, "Meta warns that election backlash against data centers could make AI expansion harder" (November 5)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/05/metas-warns-that-election-backlash-against-data-centers-could-make-ai-expansion-harder/); [CNBC, data center ballot measures (November 4)](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/04/data-center-ballot-measures-2026-midterm.html); [recording of the conference talk, linked by TechCrunch and not watched by us](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P_KTZpMqrwk).*

### Airbnb Says It Is Building Its Own AI Agent, Due in the First Half of 2027

**What happened.** Airbnb's third-quarter results release, dated November 5, reports revenue of $4.9 billion, up 19%, gross booking value of $27.7 billion, up 21%, net income of $1.9 billion and adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a profit measure) of $2.5 billion. It authorized an additional buyback of up to $12 billion. It guided fourth-quarter revenue to $3.22 billion to $3.28 billion and now expects full-year 2026 revenue growth in the high teens. On AI, it says it plans to begin rolling out its own travel agent in the first half of 2027, and states: "We don't believe people will use one AI agent for everything." TechCrunch adds, from the shareholder letter, that the agent will build a custom interface for each request from a library of hundreds of components such as maps, photos and itineraries, and that CEO Brian Chesky cited a figure that 80% of travelers enjoy researching trips.

**Why it matters.** Yesterday's edition reported Uber saying that only four rides were ordered through AI agents in its busiest week of October. Airbnb is making the same bet from the other side: that a specialist app, not a general-purpose assistant, keeps the customer relationship.

**Business implication.** The release gives the argument for owning the booking, the reservation changes and the support, which are the parts an outside agent cannot easily replace. A launch date in mid-2027 is a plan, and nothing has shipped. This is informational, not investment advice.

*Sources: [Airbnb, Q3 2026 financial results](https://news.airbnb.com/airbnb-q3-2026-financial-results/); [TechCrunch, "Airbnb says it's launching an AI travel agent in the first half of 2027" (November 5)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/05/airbnb-says-its-launching-an-ai-travel-agent-in-the-first-half-of-2027/).*

### Palo Alto Networks Reportedly in Talks to Buy Clutch for $500 Million

**What happened.** The Information first reported that Palo Alto Networks is in talks to acquire Clutch Security, which makes software that controls what AI agents can reach inside a company's systems. TechCrunch, which followed up citing a person familiar with the discussions, says the reported price is $500 million in cash plus milestone-based payments. Neither company responded to TechCrunch. The reporting is The Information's and we have not seen its article. TechCrunch says Clutch's last valuation was $85 million in a $20 million Series A in January 2025, led by Lightspeed and SignalFire, and that its annual recurring revenue (the yearly value of its subscriptions) is approaching $10 million.

**Why it matters.** On those figures the price is about 50 times annual recurring revenue, according to TechCrunch. Agent security is a young category: TechCrunch counts Palo Alto's seventh acquisition this year, per PitchBook, if this closes, and notes other recent deals, including Cyera's purchase of Oasis Security for about $1 billion and Cisco's reported purchase of Astrix for roughly $400 million.

**Business implication.** Large security vendors are buying rather than building, and startups with small revenue are being priced on what agents might do. Nothing is agreed. This is informational, not investment advice.

*Sources: [The Information, original report](https://www.theinformation.com/), reporting is The Information's, paywalled, article not located by us; [TechCrunch, "Palo Alto Networks in talks to buy AI agent security startup Clutch for $500M" (November 5)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/05/palo-alto-networks-in-talks-to-buy-ai-agent-security-startup-clutch-for-500m/).*

## Model and Product Updates

**Meta puts Muse's video avatar in front of all U.S. users.** Meta's research blog introduces Muse Realtime Avatar, which animates a reference image, such as a portrait, an animal or an object, into a live avatar that talks back. The post says it streams 448-by-768 video at 25 frames per second with about 870 milliseconds of delay, and that a single GB200 chip can run 12 live sessions at once. Meta also reports that human raters preferred it over rival products from Runway and HeyGen; that is Meta's own comparison, not independent. TechCrunch reports the feature, which runs on Muse Realtime Avatar and Muse Realtime Voice, went live for all U.S. users on Thursday, on mobile, web and Mac, with the glasses and a planned wearable called Muse Charm to follow.

**Claude gets a personal finance tool.** TechCrunch reports that Anthropic is starting a beta for U.S. users on Pro, Max and Ultra plans. Users connect bank and credit card accounts through Plaid, the account-linking service, and ask Claude to total spending by category, flag duplicate charges and unexpected fees, and explain terms such as APR. Anthropic says the tool is read-only, cannot move money, does not train on those conversations and carries no ads. It can see up to two years of transactions. TechCrunch cites an Anthropic blog post that it does not link, and we could not find it, so these are Anthropic's claims as relayed. OpenAI launched similar tools in May, per TechCrunch.

**Box builds Nvidia's retrieval models into its agent search.** At Box's developer conference, Box announced Content Retrieval, which returns the passages relevant to an agent's query rather than whole documents. Nvidia's Kari Briski told TechCrunch to expect answers about four times faster, with 20% fewer errors and 90% fewer tokens. Those are projections from a vendor, not independent results. Rollout to Enterprise Advance customers begins early next year. Nvidia's own July post says its Nemotron 3 Embed 8B model ranks first on RTEB, a retrieval benchmark, as of a July 15 screenshot; that is also Nvidia's claim.

**Underdog releases a tiny open voice model.** Per TechCrunch, Woof is a text-to-speech model of about 32 megabytes that runs on most devices offline and is free to download. We found no independent quality tests.

## Regulation and Policy Watch

**Publishers sue Brave over AI data supply.** TechCrunch reports that The New York Times sued the browser maker Brave on Monday in federal court in Manhattan, and that the Daily News, Chicago Tribune and Orlando Sentinel filed a similar suit the same day. The Times alleges Brave scraped its copyrighted content and supplied it to AI companies, which the complaint names as Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI and Cohere, calling Brave a "supplier, dealer, and distributor of stolen copyrighted content." The suits say Brave built an index of more than 30 billion web pages with a crawler publishers cannot block, and also target a browser feature that bypasses paywalls. They seek an injunction and damages. Brave had not responded when TechCrunch published. These are allegations. TechCrunch gives no case numbers and we could not reach the docket, so we cannot link the complaint itself.

**Data center rules move to the ballot.** The 41 local measures CNBC counted, described above, are the clearest policy signal of the week. TechCrunch reported yesterday, and we noted, a Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez bill for a national moratorium on new data centers. We have not read the bill text. We found no new federal action on AI preemption or a new EU AI Act step in our searches today; the EU's AI Omnibus, which delays some AI Act deadlines, was reported adopted in the summer, and we did not verify its current status.

*Sources: [TechCrunch, "New York Times and other outlets sue web browser Brave over alleged content scraping" (November 2)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/02/new-york-times-and-other-outlets-sue-web-browser-brave-over-alleged-content-scraping/), complaint and docket not located by us; [CNBC, data center ballot measures (November 4)](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/04/data-center-ballot-measures-2026-midterm.html).*

## Emerging Startup Radar

**Axiom Math says its AI verified a major math library.** TechCrunch reports that Axiom, founded by 25-year-old Carina Hong, announced that its AI agents translated about 7,654 pages of the Stacks Project, an open reference on algebraic geometry, into roughly 16 million lines of Lean. Lean is a proof language that a computer checks line by line. Axiom's own site says Lean checks every proof in both parts of the library, but that one part, "StacksFrontier," is AI-written code that has not yet had human review. Columbia mathematician Peter Woit told TechCrunch he knows of nothing comparable. TechCrunch says Axiom raised a $200 million Series A led by Menlo Ventures in March and links a valuation of $1.6 billion. There is no independent check of the claim yet.

**Physicl raises $10 million.** TechCrunch reports the seed round, led by Inovia Capital, funds a simulation tool called Studio. It launches with 10,000 3D environments and 100,000 object assets so robot developers can generate synthetic training data for rare failures, such as a cup in shadow on a cluttered counter. Founder Alex de Vigan previously built the e-commerce 3D company nfinite. Its early customer is SpAItial, a world-model company.

**Discovered Materials offers $1 million for five open science problems.** TechCrunch reports the Y Combinator startup, which uses AI to search for chip materials, put up a $1 million prize pool for AI solutions to five unsolved problems, including a theory of cuprate superconductivity. The company says frontier models made little progress on them in days of autonomous work.

**Clutch and Tigris.** See the Palo Alto item above and the Dropbox item below.

## AI Infrastructure and Market Signals

**Dropbox buys Tigris Data for $350 million.** Dropbox announced on Thursday that it will acquire Tigris Data, a storage startup founded by former Uber engineers, and unveiled DBX Agent Cloud, a storage platform for people building AI models and agents. TechCrunch says it appears to be Dropbox's largest acquisition, with closing expected in the fourth quarter. DBX Agent Cloud will launch with object storage, designed to work with tools built for Amazon's S3, and later add documents, images, audio, video and file features for agents. Dropbox says the updated product has reached more than 70,000 teams, and told TechCrunch it has no plans to cut Tigris staff. We could not open Dropbox's press release, which TechCrunch links.

**Power, again.** CNBC reports that OpenAI has secured 8 gigawatts of computing capacity at the PORTS-Pike campus in Ohio, at a total cost that could exceed $500 billion. Ohio is where 21 local measures were on the ballot. That is CNBC's figure; we have not seen OpenAI's.
